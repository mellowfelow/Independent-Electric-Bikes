A second-hand e-bike can save you 30–60 % off the new price. But it can also leave you with a dead battery, a worn drivetrain, and no warranty. Here is what to check before you hand over the money.

## Battery Health — The Most Important Check

The battery is the most expensive component on the bike ($500–$1,500 to replace) and the one most likely to be degraded on a used bike.

### What to Ask the Seller
1. **How old is the battery?** Lithium-ion batteries degrade with time and use. A battery older than 4–5 years is likely to have lost 20–40 % of its original capacity, even with moderate use.
2. **How many charge cycles?** Some displays show total charge cycles or total distance. More than 500 cycles means the battery is in the second half of its useful life.
3. **How was it stored?** A battery stored fully charged in a hot garage degrades faster than one stored at 50 % in a cool room.
4. **Has it been replaced?** If the battery is newer than the bike, that is a good sign — but verify it is compatible (correct voltage, BMS communication, original brand).

### What to Check Yourself
1. **Physical condition:** inspect the battery case for dents, cracks, swelling (bulging), or water damage. Any of these is a deal-breaker.
2. **Charge it and ride:** charge the battery to 100 %, then ride at a moderate pace on flat ground. Note the distance when the battery dies. Compare to the manufacturer's rated range. If you get less than 60 % of the rated range, the battery needs replacing soon — factor $500–$1,000 into the price.
3. **Check the charge indicator:** does the battery hold its charge level steadily, or does it jump around (showing 80 % then dropping to 40 %)? Erratic charge display indicates cell imbalance — a sign of advanced degradation.

### Battery Red Flags
- Seller cannot or will not let you test the battery
- Battery is not the original (unbranded aftermarket replacement)
- Battery is visibly swollen or damaged
- Seller says "the battery just needs a few charge cycles to come good" (it does not)

## Motor — Listen and Feel

### What to Check
1. **Listen:** ride the bike and listen to the motor. Normal sounds are a quiet hum (hub motor) or a soft gear whine (mid-drive). Abnormal sounds are grinding, clicking, knocking, or loud whining.
2. **Feel:** does the motor engage smoothly when you pedal, or does it jerk and surge? Jerky engagement suggests a worn torque sensor, faulty controller, or motor bearing issue.
3. **All assist levels:** test every assist level. If one level does not work or feels different from the others, the controller or sensor may be faulty.
4. **Motor cut-off at brakes:** pull the brake levers. The motor should cut off immediately. If it does not, the brake sensors are faulty — a safety issue.

### Motor Red Flags
- Grinding or knocking noises
- Motor does not engage on one or more assist levels
- Motor does not cut off when braking
- Visible damage to the motor housing

## Drivetrain — Chain, Cassette, Chainring

The drivetrain wears faster on an e-bike than a regular bike. On a mid-drive e-bike, the motor puts extra load on the chain.

### What to Check
1. **Chain wear:** use a chain checker tool. If the chain is at 0.75 % or more wear, it needs replacing immediately ($30–$60), and the cassette likely needs replacing too ($50–$150).
2. **Cassette condition:** look at the cassette teeth. Worn teeth are pointed and shark-finned. New teeth are flat-topped and symmetrical. A worn cassette means chain skipping under load — dangerous on hills.
3. **Chainring condition:** check the front chainring teeth for the same shark-fin wear pattern.
4. **Shifting:** shift through all gears. Smooth, quiet shifts = good condition. Crunching, skipping, or hesitation = worn components or misadjusted derailleur.

### Cost to Fix
- New chain: $30–$60
- New cassette: $50–$150
- New chainring: $40–$120
- Derailleur adjustment: $20–$40 at a bike shop

If all three need replacing, add $120–$330 to the bike's effective cost.

## Brakes

### What to Check
1. **Pad thickness:** look at the brake pads through the caliper. If the friction material is less than 1 mm thick, they need replacing ($20–$40 per pair).
2. **Lever feel:** hydraulic brakes should feel firm and engage progressively. If the lever pulls to the handlebar with little resistance (spongy), the brakes need bleeding ($30–$60 at a shop).
3. **Rotor condition:** look at the brake rotors. They should be flat, shiny, and smooth. Warped rotors (you will hear a rubbing sound when the wheel spins) need replacing ($30–$60 each).
4. **Noise:** squealing brakes may indicate contaminated pads (oil or grease on the pads) — pads need replacing.

## Frame and Fork

### What to Check
1. **Cracks:** inspect the frame at high-stress points — head tube/downtube junction, seat tube/top tube junction, bottom bracket shell, dropout area. Any crack is a deal-breaker.
2. **Dents:** small dents in steel or aluminium tubes may be cosmetic. Large dents or creases weaken the frame — avoid.
3. **Corrosion:** surface rust on a steel frame is cosmetic. Deep rust that pits the metal weakens the frame. Aluminium frames do not rust but can corrode at dissimilar-metal junctions (e.g., where a steel bolt contacts an aluminium frame).
4. **Headset:** lift the front wheel and turn the handlebars. They should turn smoothly with no clicking, grinding, or notchy feeling. A rough headset costs $30–$80 to replace.
5. **Suspension:** if the bike has a suspension fork, compress it and release. It should compress and rebound smoothly. A fork that clunks, sticks, or has no damping needs servicing ($80–$200).

## Wheels and Tyres

### What to Check
1. **Wheel trueness:** spin each wheel and watch the rim pass the brake pads. If the rim wobbles side to side more than 2 mm, the wheel needs truing ($20–$40 at a shop).
2. **Spoke tension:** squeeze pairs of spokes on each wheel. They should all feel similarly tight. Loose spokes mean the wheel is at risk of going out of true or collapsing.
3. **Tyre condition:** check tread depth, sidewall cracks, and embedded objects. Worn tyres cost $40–$80 each to replace.
4. **Hub bearings:** grab the top of each wheel and rock it side to side. Any play (clicking, looseness) means the hub bearings are worn ($30–$80 to replace).

## Electronics and Display

### What to Check
1. **Display:** does it turn on? Does it show speed, battery level, assist mode, and distance correctly? Dead pixels, flickering, or a non-responsive display costs $100–$400 to replace.
2. **Lights:** if the bike has integrated lights, check that both front and rear work.
3. **Wiring:** inspect the cables for cuts, exposed wires, or damaged connectors. Water ingress through damaged cable jackets causes electrical faults.
4. **Error codes:** some motor systems display error codes on startup or during riding. Ask the seller about any error codes. Look up the codes for the specific motor system (Bosch, Shimano, Bafang error code lists are available online).

## Pricing: What to Pay

### Fair Market Value

A used e-bike should be priced based on:
- **Age:** depreciation is steep in the first 2 years (40–50 % off new price), then slows
- **Battery condition:** a bike with a degraded battery is worth $500–$1,000 less than one with a healthy battery
- **Motor system:** reputable motor brands (Bosch, Shimano, Yamaha) hold value better than no-name motors
- **Condition:** well-maintained bikes command a premium

**Rough guide:**
| Age | Good condition | Fair condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 year | 60–70 % of new price | 50–60 % |
| 2 years | 45–55 % | 35–45 % |
| 3 years | 35–45 % | 25–35 % |
| 4+ years | 25–35 % | 15–25 % |

### Red Flags on Price
- Price is significantly below market value (stolen bike or undisclosed problems)
- Seller will not meet in person or allow a test ride
- No proof of purchase (receipt, warranty card)
- Serial number has been removed or is illegible

## Where to Buy

- **Facebook Marketplace and Gumtree:** the largest secondhand markets. Meet in a public place, inspect thoroughly, test ride.
- **Bike shops (trade-ins and refurbished):** some shops sell checked and serviced used e-bikes with a short warranty. More expensive than private sale but lower risk.
- **eBay:** buyer protection exists but shipping a 25 kg e-bike is expensive and risky (damage in transit).

## Bottom Line

A used e-bike is a smart purchase if you check the battery health, motor condition, and drivetrain wear before buying. The battery is the make-or-break component — factor in $500–$1,000 for a replacement if the battery is degraded. Always test ride, inspect for frame damage, and buy from a seller who can show a receipt and service history. A well-maintained 2-year-old e-bike at 50 % of the new price is a great deal.
