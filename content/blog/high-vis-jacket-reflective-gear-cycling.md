Being seen is a large part of being safe on an e-bike. Drivers who are tired, distracted or looking into low sun can easily miss a cyclist, and e-bikes often travel faster than people expect. High-vis and reflective gear will not make you invisible-proof, but it improves your chances of being noticed early. This guide explains when high-vis helps, how it differs from reflective gear, and what types of clothing and accessories are worth considering.

## High-Vis vs Reflective: Two Different Jobs

These terms often get lumped together, but they work differently.

**Fluorescent (high-vis) materials**
- Bright yellow, orange and lime colours stand out strongly in daylight, at dawn and at dusk.
- They work by converting some invisible light into visible light, making colours seem to glow.
- They do little at night, because they need daylight to work.

**Retroreflective materials**
- Reflect light from headlights straight back toward its source.
- Work at night, when a car's lights hit the material.
- Need a light source to work, and they do little in unlit conditions.

The best gear combines both: a fluorescent base for daytime and retroreflective panels for night. If you ride at various times of day, look for clothing that offers both.

## When High-Vis Helps Most

High-vis is not just for winter nights. It can help in many situations:

- **Dawn and dusk,** when the light is low and shadows hide riders.
- **Overcast, rainy or foggy days,** when visibility drops.
- **Busy roads and intersections,** where drivers have a lot to scan.
- **Roads with strong sun glare,** common on east-west routes at sunrise and sunset.
- **Shared paths,** where other riders and pedestrians need to notice you coming.
- **Under trees and in tunnels,** where light changes fast.

On a bright, clear midday ride on a quiet path, you may feel it is less necessary, but many commuters wear it all year round for consistency. Riders often notice drivers give them more room when wearing bright clothing, though results will vary and nothing replaces good road positioning and lights.

## Types of Gear

### High-Vis Jackets

A jacket is the most visible single item because it covers your torso. Look for:

- **Fluorescent colour** over a large area.
- **Reflective strips** on the chest, back and arms. Strips on moving parts like arms and legs draw attention because motion is recognisable to drivers.
- **A longer back hem** to cover you when you are leaning forward.
- **Ventilation,** such as zips and mesh panels, for Australian summers.
- **Waterproof or windproof fabric** if you ride in the rain.
- **A fit that works over your other clothes** without flapping.

Lightweight options suit hot weather, while insulated or waterproof jackets suit southern winters.

### Vests and Sashes

- Easy to carry and put on over normal clothes.
- Good for people who commute in work clothes.
- Usually light enough to fold into a bag.
- Useful for kids and anyone who only needs high-vis occasionally.

### Reflective Accessories

- **Ankle bands** and **leg straps** highlight the up-and-down motion of pedalling, which makes you look like a person on a bike.
- **Wrist bands** and **gloves with reflective details** help show hand signals.
- **Helmet covers** and **helmet reflective stickers** add visibility at head height.
- **Backpack covers** and **reflective bag tags** make you more visible from behind.
- **Reflective tape** on your frame, mudguards or forks can be added cheaply.

### Pants, Shoes and Socks

Some trousers and shoes include reflective panels. These are especially effective because they move as you pedal.

## Adding Lights to the Mix

Clothing is only one layer. A front and rear light are much more important at night, and many jurisdictions require them. Gear that bounces light back does not replace a light that makes its own. See our [bike light guide](/blog/bike-light-lumens-guide-front-rear-sets/) for help choosing lights, and combine them with clothing so you are visible from the front, sides and rear.

## Comfort, Fit and Practicality

A high-vis jacket you hate wearing will end up in the cupboard. Consider:

- **Breathability.** Look for fabrics that let heat escape.
- **Pockets,** for keys, cards and a phone.
- **Packability,** especially if you only need it for part of the trip.
- **Wash care.** Fluorescent dyes fade over time, so follow washing instructions and replace gear when the colour dulls or the reflective strips peel.
- **Sizing.** Make sure you can ride in a natural position without the sleeves riding up.

## High-Vis for Different Riders

- **Commuters:** a jacket or vest worn over work clothes is the most practical approach.
- **Delivery riders:** gear that is visible from all angles and holds up to constant use. Check any requirements from your employer or platform.
- **Families:** kids benefit from bright clothing and reflective accessories, particularly when riding to and from school in low light.
- **Weekend riders:** a lightweight vest or bright jersey is a simple way to improve visibility on bush or coastal roads.

## Rules and Standards

In general, Australian road rules require lights and reflectors on bikes at night, but they do not require high-vis clothing for private riders. Some workplaces and delivery jobs set their own requirements. Standards exist for high-visibility workwear, but they are written for occupational use. If you want to know whether a piece of gear meets a particular standard, check the label or ask the manufacturer.

## Limits of High-Vis

High-vis is helpful, not magical. It will not help if a driver is looking at their phone, if you are in a blind spot, or if you make unpredictable moves. Good habits matter as much as good gear: ride predictably, signal clearly, make eye contact with drivers where you can, avoid blind spots of large vehicles and use lights at every opportunity.
