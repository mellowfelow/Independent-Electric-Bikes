Choosing a mobility scooter is a big decision, and it is one that affects your independence, safety, and daily routine for years. The right scooter lets you get to the shops, visit friends, and enjoy the outdoors without relying on lifts or worrying about distance. The wrong one ends up parked in the garage because it is too big to store, too hard to steer, or does not suit the places you actually go. This guide walks through the main types of mobility scooter available in Australia, the features that matter, and how funding such as the NDIS may fit in.

## Start With How You Will Actually Use It

Before comparing models, write down where the scooter will go. A scooter for short trips around a retirement village has very different needs from one used for shopping centres, footpaths, and weekend outings.

Ask yourself:

- **Where will I ride most?** Smooth indoor floors, suburban footpaths, parks, or rougher ground?
- **How far is a typical day?** Short hops to the corner shop or several hours out?
- **How will I store it?** A garage, a unit with a lift, or a spot in the car boot?
- **Will I transport it?** In a car, on a ramp, or not at all?
- **Who else might use it?** A carer may need to lift or load it.

Being honest about these answers narrows the field quickly.

## The Main Types of Mobility Scooter

### Three-wheel scooters

Three-wheelers have a single front wheel and a tighter turning circle. That makes them well suited to indoor use, narrow aisles, and crowded shopping centres. Many riders find them nimble and easy to manoeuvre in tight spaces. The trade-off is that they can feel less stable on slopes, uneven ground, and when turning at speed, so they suit lighter, smoother-surface use.

### Four-wheel scooters

Four-wheelers have a wider, more stable base and generally feel more planted outdoors. They are a popular all-rounder for people who spend time on footpaths and mixed surfaces. They usually have a larger turning circle than a three-wheeler, so check that you can comfortably turn in your hallway, lift, or garage.

### Travel and folding scooters

Travel scooters are built to be compact and light, with designs that fold or split into sections so they fit into a car boot or can be carried more easily. They are ideal for holidays, cruises, and regular car trips, though they typically trade some range, comfort, and weight capacity for portability. See our [travel and folding mobility scooter guide](/blog/travel-folding-mobility-scooter-guide/) for more detail.

### Heavy-duty and all-terrain scooters

Larger scooters are built with stronger frames, bigger wheels, better suspension, and higher weight capacities. They suit riders who want extra stability, who use their scooter outdoors on rougher surfaces, or who need a higher load rating. They are heavier and bulkier, so think about storage and transport. Read our [heavy-duty and all-terrain scooter guide](/blog/heavy-duty-mobility-scooter-all-terrain-guide/) for the full picture.

## Key Features to Compare

### Weight capacity

Every scooter has a maximum user weight, and it is a safety limit, not a suggestion. Choose a scooter with a rating comfortably above your weight plus anything you regularly carry, such as shopping or a bag. Running close to the limit reduces performance, shortens battery life, and increases wear.

### Range

Range figures supplied by manufacturers are measured under ideal conditions. Hills, rough surfaces, rider weight, cold weather, and frequent stopping and starting all reduce how far you will really go. Pick a scooter with a comfortable margin over your longest typical trip so you are never worried about getting home. Our [battery, range and charging tips](/blog/mobility-scooter-battery-range-charging-tips/) cover how to get the most from each charge.

### Seat comfort and adjustability

You may be sitting for a long time, so look for a padded seat that swivels (making it easier to get on and off), adjustable armrests, and a tiller that adjusts for reach and angle. Try different models if you can, as comfort is very personal.

### Suspension and tyres

Suspension and larger wheels smooth out cracks, kerbs, and rough paths. Pneumatic (air-filled) tyres generally give a more comfortable ride but can puncture, while solid tyres need less maintenance but feel firmer.

### Ground clearance and kerb climbing

If your local footpaths have steep driveways or kerb ramps, ground clearance matters. Ask the retailer how a model copes with the type of surfaces you will meet.

### Turning circle and manoeuvrability

Measure the tightest space you need to turn in at home and compare it against the scooter's turning circle.

### Controls and display

Look for clear, easy-to-read displays and controls that suit your hand strength and dexterity. Speed dials, forward and reverse levers, and braking systems should feel intuitive and be comfortable to use for long periods.

### Lights, horn, and indicators

Lights, a horn, and reflectors improve visibility, particularly around dusk. Some scooters add indicators and a rear-view mirror, which are useful for busier areas.

## Weight and Transport

A scooter that is too heavy to lift or load defeats the purpose. If you plan to carry it in a car, check the heaviest single component and whether the scooter breaks down into sections. If you rely on a ramp or hoist, make sure it is compatible with the scooter and rated for the weight. If you store the scooter in a unit or townhouse, confirm that it fits through doorways, lifts, and common areas.

## Funding and the NDIS

For some people, mobility scooters may be funded or part-funded through support schemes. If you are an NDIS participant, a scooter may be considered as assistive technology, depending on your plan and goals. Funding decisions depend on your individual circumstances, your plan, and what is considered reasonable and necessary, so there is no one-size-fits-all answer.

In general terms:

- Talk to your NDIS planner or support coordinator before buying.
- An assessment from an occupational therapist (OT) is often part of the process for assistive technology.
- Keep quotes and written recommendations to support your request.
- Other programs, such as aged care supports or state-based equipment schemes, may also be available depending on where you live.

Rules and eligibility change, so check with your planner, your state or territory equipment program, or your aged care assessor rather than relying on general advice.

## Test Before You Commit

Wherever possible, try a scooter before you buy. Sit on it, steer it, and test the controls. Check how easily you can get on and off, how it handles a gentle slope, and how it feels at low speed. Ask about warranty, servicing, replacement batteries, and spare parts, because ongoing support matters as much as the initial purchase.

## Safety and Everyday Habits

Even the best scooter needs a careful rider. Slow down on slopes and when turning, keep to paths where you are permitted to ride, and take extra care near driveways and crossings. Learn what your scooter's controls do before heading out, and practise in a quiet area first.
