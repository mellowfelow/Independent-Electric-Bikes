Australia is not flat. Melbourne's inner suburbs have 8–12 % gradients. Sydney's North Shore climbs are brutal. Brisbane is built on hills. If your commute includes a serious climb, the wrong e-bike will overheat, cut out, or leave you pedalling a 25 kg bike with no assist.

## What Makes an E-Bike Good on Hills

Three things determine hill performance: **motor torque**, **motor type**, and **battery capacity**. Speed and wattage matter less than you think.

### Torque (Nm) — The Most Important Number

Torque is rotational force. It is what pushes you up a hill. Wattage tells you how fast the motor can sustain that push, but torque tells you how hard it pushes.

| Torque | Hill capability |
|---|---|
| 30–40 Nm | Flat terrain only. Struggles on anything above 5 % gradient. |
| 50–65 Nm | Handles moderate hills (5–10 % gradient) with pedalling |
| 70–85 Nm | Handles steep hills (10–15 % gradient) comfortably |
| 85–120 Nm | Handles very steep hills (15 %+), heavy loads, and cargo bikes on hills |

**For hilly Australian commutes, look for 65 Nm minimum.** 80+ Nm is ideal if your route includes sustained climbs.

### Mid-Drive vs Hub Motor on Hills

**Mid-drive motors** (Bosch, Shimano, Bafang, Yamaha) are mounted at the pedal crank. They drive the chain, which means they use the bike's gears. On a hill, you shift to a lower gear and the motor's force is multiplied — just like your legs. This is why mid-drives dominate in hilly terrain.

**Hub motors** are mounted in the rear wheel. They deliver power directly to the wheel at a fixed ratio — no gear multiplication. On a steep hill, a hub motor must push harder against a load it cannot gear down for. This causes:
- Higher current draw (drains battery faster)
- Heat build-up (the motor gets hot)
- Thermal cutout on steep, sustained climbs (the controller reduces power to protect the motor)

**Verdict:** for serious hills, a mid-drive motor is the right choice. Hub motors are fine for gentle gradients (under 8 %) and short climbs.

### Battery Capacity

Climbing drains the battery 2–4 times faster than riding on flat ground. A 400 Wh battery that gives you 60 km on flat terrain may only give you 25–35 km on a hilly route.

**For hilly commutes:** 500 Wh minimum, 625+ Wh recommended. If your commute is under 15 km with a big hill, 400 Wh may suffice — but you will be watching the battery gauge on cold days and headwinds.

## Gearing for Hills

### Gear Range

The bike's gear range determines the lowest gear available for climbing. A wider gear range means a lower bottom gear — less effort on steep hills.

**Key metric:** the gear ratio of the lowest gear. A 34-tooth chainring with a 51-tooth rear cog gives a ratio of 0.67:1 — very low, ideal for steep climbs. A 42-tooth chainring with a 32-tooth rear cog gives 1.31:1 — much harder to climb.

### Cassette Size

For hilly riding, look for a cassette with a large largest cog:
- **11-42T or 11-46T:** good for moderate hills
- **11-51T:** excellent for steep hills
- **11-52T (SRAM Eagle):** maximum climbing range

### Internal Hub Gears

Some e-bikes use internal hub gears (Shimano Nexus, Enviolo, Rohloff) instead of a derailleur. These are low-maintenance and shift under load — useful for stop-start hill climbing in city traffic. However, they are heavier and less efficient than derailleur systems, and the gear range is narrower on cheaper models (Nexus 3 or 5-speed).

The **Enviolo continuously variable hub** is the best option for hills in this category — infinite gear ratios within its range, silent shifting, and no chain-drop risk.

## Riding Technique on Hills

Even with a powerful motor, technique matters:

### Shift Before the Hill

Shift to a lower gear before the gradient steepens, not after. Shifting under high load (mid-climb) puts enormous stress on the chain, cassette, and derailleur. On a mid-drive e-bike, this stress is amplified by the motor's torque.

### Keep a Steady Cadence

Pedal at a consistent, moderate cadence (60–80 RPM) rather than grinding slowly in a high gear. The motor is most efficient when the rider is pedalling smoothly. Most mid-drive systems measure cadence and torque — low cadence with high torque is the least efficient combination.

### Use the Right Assist Level

On a long climb, use a moderate assist level (Eco or Tour/Normal) rather than maximum. Maximum assist drains the battery fast and may overheat the motor on sustained climbs. A moderate assist level with your own effort in a low gear is more sustainable.

### Stand When Needed

Standing on the pedals shifts your weight forward and increases the force per pedal stroke. Use this technique for short, steep sections — but do not stand for extended climbs, as it is less energy-efficient and puts more stress on the drivetrain.

### Zigzag on Very Steep Tracks

On unpaved trails or very steep fire roads (15 %+), riding a zigzag pattern reduces the effective gradient. This is standard mountain biking technique and works equally well on an e-MTB.

## Heat Management

Motor overheating is the primary limitation on sustained climbs. When the motor or controller gets too hot, the system reduces power — sometimes dramatically.

**Signs of thermal limiting:**
- Sudden drop in assist power mid-climb
- A thermometer icon on the display
- The motor feels hot to the touch at the top of a climb

**Prevention:**
- Use moderate assist levels on long climbs
- Keep a steady cadence — high torque at low RPM generates more heat
- Let the motor cool on descents (coast without assist)
- Do not cover the motor housing with bags or accessories that block airflow

## Bottom Line

For hilly Australian terrain, choose a mid-drive e-bike with 65+ Nm of torque, a 500+ Wh battery, and a wide-range cassette (11-42T minimum). Shift before the climb, maintain a steady cadence, and use moderate assist levels on long hills. Hub motors work for gentle slopes but overheat and drain the battery on sustained, steep climbs.
