Delivery riding on an e-bike is the hardest test a bike can face: 6–10 hours a day, every day, in all weather, carrying loads, with constant stop-start riding that stresses every component. Here is what works and what breaks.

## Choosing an E-Bike for Delivery

### Motor: 250 W Mid-Drive or 350+ W Hub
The legal limit in Australia is 250 W continuous / 25 km/h for a bicycle. Most delivery riders comply; some run higher-powered bikes and accept the legal risk.

**Mid-drive (250 W, 60+ Nm):** uses the bike's gears efficiently. Better on hills, better range per charge. Higher chain wear — budget for a new chain every 2,000–3,000 km.

**Hub motor (250–500 W):** simpler, cheaper to maintain (no chain stress from the motor). Less efficient on hills. Direct-drive hubs offer regenerative braking (extends range slightly on hilly routes).

### Battery: 600+ Wh (Two Batteries Ideal)
A delivery shift of 6–8 hours at medium-high assist drains 400–600 Wh. You need either:
- One large battery (600–750 Wh) and a midday charge
- Two batteries (400+ Wh each) — swap at the halfway point, charge the depleted one overnight

**Tip:** buy a second battery when you buy the bike. By the time you need it (month 3–4, when your original battery has lost 5–10 % capacity from daily deep cycling), the exact model may be discontinued.

### Frame: Steel or Heavy-Duty Aluminium
The frame takes constant load stress (bag on the rack, pothole impacts, kerb drops). A cheap aluminium frame will crack at the welds within 12–18 months of daily delivery use. Choose:
- A cargo-rated rear rack (25+ kg capacity, bolted to frame — not seatpost-mounted)
- A frame designed for touring or commuting (thicker tubing, reinforced dropouts)
- Steel if available — it handles fatigue better than aluminium for this use

### Tyres: Puncture-Resistant, 38 mm+
A flat tyre during a delivery shift costs you 20–30 minutes and $5–$15 in lost earnings. Use:
- **Schwalbe Marathon Plus** (the industry standard for flat protection)
- **Continental Contact Plus** (comparable protection, slightly lighter)
- **Width: 38–45 mm** (wider is more comfortable and more stable with a load)
- **Tubeless if your rims support it** (self-sealing sealant handles small punctures without stopping)

### Brakes: Hydraulic Disc, 180 mm Rotors
You brake hundreds of times per shift — at every delivery stop, every traffic light, every pedestrian crossing. Mechanical disc or rim brakes wear out fast and lose power as pads thin. Hydraulic disc brakes:
- Self-adjust as pads wear
- Require less hand force (less fatigue over a long shift)
- Consistent power in wet conditions

**Budget for brake pads every 2,000–3,000 km** ($15–$30 per set, 5 minutes to install).

### Lights: Always On, Bright
Delivery riding includes dawn, dusk, and night shifts. You need:
- **Front light:** 400+ lumens, steady beam (not flashing — you need to see the road, not just be seen)
- **Rear light:** 50+ lumens, visible from 200+ metres
- **Reflective elements:** on the bag, on the bike, on your clothing
- **Powered by the main battery** if possible (one fewer thing to charge)

## The Delivery Bag

### Insulated Backpack vs Rack-Mounted Box

**Backpack (Uber Eats / DoorDash style):**
- Pro: you carry it to the door — no fumbling at the bike
- Con: weight on your shoulders for 6–8 hours. Back pain, neck pain, sweat
- Con: shifts your centre of gravity higher — less stable on the bike

**Rack-mounted insulated box:**
- Pro: no weight on your body. More comfortable over long shifts
- Pro: lower centre of gravity — more stable riding
- Con: you unload at the bike and carry food to the door by hand — awkward for multi-item orders
- Con: needs a strong rack (25+ kg rated) and secure mounting

**Recommendation for full-time riders:** rack-mounted box. The comfort over a full shift is worth the minor inconvenience at each delivery. Use a small handlebar bag for drinks and small orders.

## Maintenance Schedule for Delivery Bikes

Daily delivery riding compresses maintenance intervals. What a recreational rider does annually, you do monthly.

| Task | Recreational rider | Delivery rider |
|---|---|---|
| Chain clean + lube | Every 200 km | Every 100 km (every 2–3 days) |
| Chain replacement | 3,000–5,000 km | 2,000–3,000 km |
| Brake pad replacement | 5,000–8,000 km | 2,000–3,000 km |
| Tyre replacement | 5,000–10,000 km | 3,000–5,000 km |
| Brake bleed | Annually | Every 6 months |
| Full service | Annually | Every 3–4 months |
| Battery replacement | 3–5 years | 1–2 years (daily deep cycling) |

### Weekly Check (5 Minutes)
1. Tyre pressure (correct pressure prevents pinch flats and improves range)
2. Brake pad thickness (replace below 1 mm)
3. Chain wear (check with a chain wear tool — replace at 0.5 % elongation)
4. Bolt tightness (rack bolts, stem bolts, axle bolts — vibration loosens them)
5. Light function (front, rear, indicators if fitted)

## Earning More Per Hour

### Route Efficiency
The motor helps you ride faster between deliveries, but the biggest time savings come from:
- **Knowing your area:** which streets are one-way, where the shortcuts are, which buildings have difficult access
- **Stacking orders:** multi-drop deliveries on the same route save travel time
- **Positioning between orders:** wait in the cluster of restaurants, not at home

### Battery Management
Running out of battery mid-shift means pedalling a 22 kg bike with no assist — your delivery rate drops by 30–50 %. Manage the battery:
- Use Eco or Tour mode on flat ground (save Turbo for hills)
- Keep a spare battery charged
- Know where you can quick-charge (some restaurants will let you plug in)

### Peak Hours
Most platforms pay surge rates during peak demand:
- Lunch: 11:30–13:30
- Dinner: 17:30–20:30
- Rain: any time it rains (fewer riders = higher demand = surge pricing)

Ride during peaks, rest during lulls. A focused 5-hour strategy (covering both peaks) can earn more than an unfocused 8-hour shift.

### Weather Riding
Rain, wind, and cold reduce the number of active riders — which increases demand and often triggers surge pricing. Riders who are comfortable in bad weather earn significantly more.

Invest in waterproof gear (jacket, trousers, shoe covers, gloves). The $200 outlay pays for itself in a week of wet-weather surges.

## Safety

### Visibility
You ride in traffic, in all conditions, carrying a load that affects handling. Be visible:
- High-visibility vest or jacket (always, not just at night)
- Lights on (always, even in daylight)
- Reflective elements on the bag

### Road Position
Ride predictably. Signal turns. Do not weave between cars. Take the lane when it is too narrow for a car to pass safely. Do not ride on the footpath (illegal in most Australian states, and pedestrians are unpredictable).

### Fatigue
Riding 6–8 hours a day is physically demanding. Fatigue reduces reaction time and decision-making. Take breaks, eat properly, stay hydrated, and stop when you are too tired to ride safely. No delivery is worth a crash.

## Tax Deductions (Australia)

As a delivery rider using your own e-bike, you can claim:
- **E-bike purchase** (depreciation over the effective life — typically 3–4 years, or instant write-off for assets under the threshold)
- **Repairs and maintenance** (parts, labour, tyres, brake pads, chains)
- **Accessories** (lights, rack, bag, phone mount, lock)
- **Phone and data** (the proportion used for the delivery app)
- **Protective gear** (helmet, gloves, high-vis clothing)

Keep receipts for everything. Use a logbook for the first 12 weeks to establish your work-use percentage. Consult a tax agent familiar with gig economy deductions.

## Bottom Line

A delivery e-bike needs to be tough, reliable, and cheap to maintain. Choose puncture-resistant tyres, hydraulic disc brakes, a 600+ Wh battery (or two), and a cargo-rated rack. Maintain the bike aggressively — a chain clean every 2–3 days, pad check weekly, full service every 3–4 months. Ride during peak hours, manage your battery, know your area, and invest in wet-weather gear. A well-set-up e-bike and a smart strategy can earn $25–$40/hour in Australian capital cities.
