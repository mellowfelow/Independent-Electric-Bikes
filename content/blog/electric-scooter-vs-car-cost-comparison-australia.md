For short trips in an Australian city, the car is often the default simply because it is already in the driveway. But when you add up what a car costs to own and run, compared with an electric scooter, the picture can look very different. This guide gives you a framework to work out your own numbers. We have deliberately not used made-up averages, because your costs depend on your car, your commute, your state and your electricity plan. Grab a pen, your last few bills and a calculator, and fill in the blanks.

## The Two Types of Cost

Every form of transport has two kinds of cost.

- **Fixed (ownership) costs:** what you pay whether or not you use it. Purchase, registration, insurance, depreciation.
- **Variable (running) costs:** what you pay per trip or per kilometre. Fuel or electricity, tyres, servicing, parking, tolls.

A fair comparison looks at both, ideally over a full year, and then divides by how many trips you actually take.

## Car Costs to Include

Work through each of these using real figures from your own paperwork and bank statements.

- **Fuel:** Check your fuel receipts or card statements for the last few months. Multiply by twelve if you want an annual figure, or work out the cost per kilometre for the specific trip you would replace.
- **Registration:** Varies by state and vehicle type. Look at your most recent rego renewal notice. Do not forget compulsory third-party insurance, which in many states is bundled with or billed alongside registration.
- **Insurance:** Comprehensive or third-party property cover, from your renewal notice.
- **Servicing and repairs:** Logbook services, brakes, batteries, and the occasional unexpected repair. Look at the past year's invoices.
- **Tyres:** A full set is a significant periodic expense. Divide the cost by the number of years you expect it to last.
- **Parking:** Daily parking fees, monthly permits, or the cost of paying for a spot at home if you live in an apartment. In central Melbourne, Sydney or Brisbane, this line can dominate the whole comparison.
- **Tolls:** Particularly relevant in Sydney and Melbourne. Check your e-tag statements.
- **Fines and parking infringements:** Include them if they are a regular part of your life.
- **Depreciation:** Cars lose value over time. Estimate this as the difference between what you could sell the car for now and what you could sell it for in a year.
- **Finance interest:** If you are paying off a car loan, include the interest.

## Electric Scooter Costs to Include

The same exercise applies to a scooter, though the line items are shorter and usually smaller.

- **Purchase price:** The upfront cost of the scooter, plus a helmet, lights, lock and any other accessories. Spread it over how many years you expect to use it.
- **Electricity:** Check your power bill for your rate per kilowatt hour and look in the scooter's specifications for its battery capacity. Multiplying the two gives an approximate cost of a full charge. Remember that charging is not perfectly efficient, so allow a little extra.
- **Tyres and tubes:** Consumables that wear out. Check retail prices for the size your scooter uses.
- **Brake pads and discs:** Another wear item. Prices vary by scooter type.
- **Servicing and repairs:** Budget for the occasional repair, a technician's time or replacement parts.
- **Battery replacement:** All lithium batteries lose capacity over time. If you plan to keep the scooter for many years, set aside a sum for an eventual replacement.
- **Insurance:** Optional, but some riders take out contents or specific cover for theft and liability. Check what your existing policies include and ask your insurer.
- **Registration:** Personal e-scooters that comply with local rules are generally not registered in the way a car is. Rules vary and are still evolving, so check your state road authority.
- **Parking:** Usually zero, though you may want a secure place to lock or store it, or a charging spot at work.
- **Depreciation:** Like any product, a scooter loses resale value. Use the second-hand market to get a feel for what a similar model is worth after a year or two.

## Build Your Own Comparison

Use a simple table with two columns, car and scooter, and a row for each item above. Fill in annual costs wherever you can. Then:

1. **Add up each column** to get a yearly total.
2. **Estimate how many trips** you would replace per week, and multiply by the weeks you ride per year. Be honest about weather and holidays.
3. **Divide the annual total by the number of trips** to get a cost per trip for each option.
4. **Do a second version** based on trips only, ignoring fixed costs, if you plan to keep the car regardless. This answers the question "what do I save by leaving the car at home for this journey?"

That second version matters. If you will keep the car for weekend and long-distance use anyway, then registration and insurance are not saved by buying a scooter, and the real saving is fuel, parking, tolls and wear on the car. If a scooter lets your household drop to one car instead of two, the savings are much bigger, because the whole fixed cost disappears.

## Costs That Are Easy to Forget

- **Time:** Parking, traffic and walking from a car park all take time. Door-to-door, a scooter can be competitive for short trips, particularly in congested areas. Time your own journey both ways before assuming.
- **Weather:** On wet or very hot days, will you realistically still ride? If not, you may still need car access or public transport, and that has a cost.
- **Carrying capacity:** A scooter is no good for school runs, shopping or tools. Factor in how often you need to carry something heavy.
- **Safety gear:** A good helmet, lights and appropriate clothing are real costs and are not optional.
- **Health and wellbeing:** Hard to put a dollar figure on, but riding outdoors is a different experience from sitting in traffic.
- **Theft risk:** Scooters can be stolen. A quality lock and secure storage are part of the cost.

## Is the Scooter Always Cheaper?

Not necessarily. For long commutes, poor weather, steep terrain, or trips with passengers and cargo, a car or public transport may be the better choice. A scooter is strongest on short, repeated urban trips of a few kilometres, where parking is costly or scarce, and where there is safe, legal infrastructure to ride on. The aim of this exercise is not to prove the scooter wins, but to give you honest numbers so you can decide.

## Check the Rules Before You Commit

Part of the cost comparison is whether your intended route is legal and safe. E-scooter rules differ between states and territories, covering where you can ride, speed limits, helmets, age limits and power limits.
