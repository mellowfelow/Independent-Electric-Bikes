Suspension on an e-bike is not just about comfort — it keeps the tyre in contact with the ground over bumps, which means better traction, better braking, and better control. But suspension only works properly if it is set up for your weight and riding style. A fork that is too stiff or too soft is worse than no suspension at all.

## Do You Need Suspension?

**You need front suspension if:**
- You ride on rough roads, cracked footpaths, or gravel
- You ride off-road (trails, fire roads, singletrack)
- You commute over tram tracks, speed bumps, and potholes
- You have wrist, shoulder, or back issues aggravated by road vibration

**You do not need suspension if:**
- You ride exclusively on smooth, sealed roads
- You want the lightest, most efficient bike possible
- You ride a road-style e-bike with drop bars

**You need rear suspension if:**
- You ride technical off-road trails
- You ride a cargo bike over rough surfaces with heavy loads
- You want maximum comfort on very rough commutes

For most Australian commuters, **a front suspension fork plus a suspension seatpost** provides 90 % of the comfort benefit of full suspension at a fraction of the weight, cost, and maintenance.

## Setting Up a Front Suspension Fork

### Step 1: Identify Your Fork Type

**Air forks** use compressed air as the spring. Adjustable for rider weight by changing air pressure. Found on mid-range and premium e-bikes.

**Coil forks** use a metal spring. Spring rate is fixed (unless you swap the spring for a stiffer or softer one). Found on budget and some heavy-duty e-bikes.

### Step 2: Set the Sag (Air Forks Only)

Sag is how much the fork compresses under your static weight — when you sit on the bike without moving. Correct sag ensures the fork can extend into dips (rebound) as well as compress into bumps.

**Target sag by riding style:**
- **Commuting:** 15–20 % of total travel
- **Trail riding:** 20–25 % of total travel
- **Enduro/aggressive:** 25–30 % of total travel

**How to measure sag:**
1. Set the fork to fully open (no lockout)
2. Push the rubber O-ring on the stanchion down to the dust seal
3. Sit on the bike in your normal riding position (feet on pedals, hands on bars)
4. Carefully dismount without bouncing the fork
5. Measure the distance the O-ring moved from the dust seal
6. Divide by total travel to get the sag percentage

**Adjusting sag:** add air pressure (with a shock pump, not a floor pump) to reduce sag (stiffer). Release air to increase sag (softer). Adjust in 5 PSI increments and re-measure.

### Step 3: Set the Rebound

Rebound controls how fast the fork returns to its extended position after compressing. Too fast and the fork bounces like a pogo stick, throwing you off balance. Too slow and the fork stays compressed (packs down), leaving no travel for the next bump.

**Setting rebound:**
1. Find the rebound dial (usually a red knob at the bottom of the fork leg)
2. Start with the dial at the midpoint
3. Ride over a speed bump or kerb at moderate speed
4. If the fork bounces back harshly (kicks up), slow the rebound (turn dial clockwise)
5. If the fork feels sluggish and stays compressed, speed the rebound (turn dial counter-clockwise)
6. The correct setting is when the fork returns smoothly to full extension without bouncing

### Step 4: Lockout (If Available)

A lockout switch stiffens or locks the fork for smooth roads. This eliminates pedal bob (fork compressing under pedalling force), making climbing and sprinting more efficient.

**Use lockout when:**
- Climbing a long, smooth hill
- Riding on smooth sealed roads at speed
- You want maximum efficiency and do not need bump absorption

**Release lockout when:**
- The road gets rough
- You approach potholes or tram tracks
- You start descending

Some forks have a threshold lockout (opens automatically above a certain impact force). This is the best option — you can leave it locked and it unlocks automatically when you hit a big bump.

## Suspension Seatpost

A suspension seatpost replaces your standard seatpost and adds 20–40 mm of travel at the saddle. It absorbs bumps that the rear of the bike transmits through the frame — the hits that the front fork does not help with.

**Types:**
- **Elastomer:** uses a rubber or gel insert. Simple, maintenance-free, limited travel. Good for light bumps.
- **Air/oil (parallelogram):** uses an air spring and oil damper in a parallelogram linkage. The saddle moves vertically (not in an arc), which feels natural. More travel, adjustable for rider weight. The Thudbuster and Suntour NCX are the benchmarks.
- **Coil:** a metal spring inside the seatpost. Heavier but durable. Less adjustable than air.

**Setting up a suspension seatpost:**
1. Adjust the spring preload for your weight (most seatposts have an adjustment dial or Allen bolt)
2. Sit on the bike — the seatpost should compress about 20–30 % of its travel under your static weight
3. Ride over a speed bump — the saddle should absorb the bump without bottoming out

## Suspension and E-Bike Weight

E-bikes are heavier than regular bikes (20–30 kg vs 10–15 kg). This affects suspension setup:

- **You need more spring rate** (higher air pressure or stiffer coil) than a regular cyclist of the same weight, because the bike's extra weight contributes to the suspended mass
- **Add the bike's weight to your body weight** when looking up manufacturer pressure charts. A 75 kg rider on a 25 kg e-bike is a 100 kg system weight for suspension purposes.
- **Budget forks may not handle the weight.** Cheap coil forks designed for 60–80 kg riders on 12 kg bikes may bottom out constantly under a 100 kg e-bike system weight. Upgrade to an air fork or a coil fork rated for higher weight.

## Maintenance

### Air Forks

- Check pressure monthly (air leaks slowly past the seals)
- Clean and lube the stanchions (the shiny upper tubes) every 50 hours of riding with a silicone spray or dedicated fork oil
- Full service (seal and oil change) every 100–200 hours or annually

### Coil Forks

- Clean and lube the stanchions regularly
- Check for play in the bushings (rock the wheel side to side — any clunking means the bushings need replacing)
- Coil springs last years with no maintenance

### Suspension Seatposts

- Minimal maintenance — wipe clean and check for play every few months
- Elastomer inserts harden over time and may need replacing every 2–3 years

## Bottom Line

Suspension makes rough roads comfortable and keeps your tyres planted for better grip and braking. But it only works if it is set up correctly for your weight. Spend 15 minutes adjusting sag and rebound when you first get the bike, check air pressure monthly, and clean the stanchions regularly. A well-set-up $150 air fork outperforms a poorly-set-up $500 fork every time.
