Australian summers mean 30–45 °C days, UV extremes, and the question every potential e-bike commuter asks: will I arrive at work drenched in sweat?

The short answer: on an e-bike, much less than you think.

## Why E-Bikes Are Different in Heat

On a regular bike at 20 km/h, you generate 75–150 W of sustained power from your legs. That is a moderate workout — enough to raise your core temperature and produce significant sweat in hot conditions.

On an e-bike at the same speed, you generate 30–60 W. The motor does the rest. That is a gentle effort — closer to walking pace exertion than cycling. You still generate heat, but far less.

**The key factors:**
- **Assist level:** higher assist = less effort = less sweat. On Turbo/High assist, you are barely working.
- **Speed:** airflow at 20–25 km/h provides significant cooling. You are creating your own breeze.
- **Terrain:** hills are the sweat-makers. On flat terrain with high assist, sweating is minimal. On a steep climb with low assist, you will sweat regardless of the e-bike.

## Strategies for Arriving Fresh

### Use High Assist

This is the simplest solution. Riding on Turbo or High assist reduces your physical output to the point where sweating is minimal — even in 35 °C heat. The trade-off is battery range: high assist drains the battery 2–3 times faster than Eco. But for a 10 km commute on a 500 Wh battery, range is not a concern even on maximum assist.

### Time Your Ride

Temperature peaks between 2–5 PM. Morning commutes (7–9 AM) are often 5–15 °C cooler than the afternoon peak. If your work hours are flexible, leaving early and finishing early avoids the worst heat.

### Dress Light, Change at Work

- **Ride in lightweight, breathable clothing** (moisture-wicking fabric, not cotton). Cycling-specific jerseys are designed for this. A lightweight merino wool t-shirt is the best all-rounder — it wicks moisture and does not smell.
- **Carry work clothes** in a pannier or bag (not a backpack — a backpack traps heat against your back, which is the largest sweat zone). A rear rack and pannier keeps the weight off your body.
- **Change at work.** Even if you barely sweat on the ride, changing your shirt and freshening up is worth the 2 minutes.

### Use a Towel and Deodorant

Keep a small towel, deodorant, and a spare shirt at work. This is the minimum kit for a summer e-bike commuter. A face wipe or wet washcloth handles the post-ride cool-down.

### Take the Shady Route

If your route has options, choose the one with more shade — tree-lined streets, covered bike paths, or routes along waterways (which are typically a few degrees cooler). A shady route may be 500 m longer but 5 °C cooler.

### Hydrate Before and During

Drink 500 ml of water in the 30 minutes before riding. Carry a water bottle on the bike (most e-bikes have a bottle cage mount). Dehydration raises your core temperature faster and triggers heavier sweating.

## Protecting the Battery in Heat

High temperatures damage lithium-ion batteries. Heat accelerates chemical degradation of the cells, permanently reducing capacity.

### Temperature Limits
- **Riding:** safe up to 45 °C ambient temperature (the motor and battery generate additional heat, so internal temperatures may be 10–15 °C higher than ambient)
- **Charging:** charge only between 5–40 °C. Never charge a battery that is hot from riding — let it cool for 30 minutes first
- **Storage:** keep the battery below 30 °C when stored. In an Australian summer, a car boot or uncovered garage can reach 50–60 °C — enough to permanently degrade the battery in a few hours

### Summer Battery Tips
- **Do not leave the bike in direct sunlight** for extended periods. Park in shade or bring the battery inside.
- **Remove the battery from the bike** if leaving the bike in a hot location (car rack, outdoor parking, uncovered garage). Store the battery indoors in a climate-controlled room.
- **Charge after the battery cools down** — not immediately after a ride. A hot battery charged hot degrades faster.
- **Do not charge overnight** in summer if the charging area is not ventilated. Charging generates heat, and a poorly ventilated space can trap enough heat to create unsafe conditions.

## Sun Protection for the Rider

### UV Exposure

Australia has some of the highest UV levels in the world. A 30-minute e-bike commute in summer exposes you to significant UV radiation.

- **Sunscreen:** SPF 50+ on exposed skin (face, neck, arms, hands). Apply 20 minutes before riding. Reapply at lunchtime if riding home in the afternoon.
- **UV-rated sunglasses:** protect your eyes from UV and from wind, insects, and road debris. Wrap-around styles with clear or amber lenses are best for cycling.
- **Helmet peak or visor:** shades your forehead and reduces glare. Some commuter helmets have built-in visors.
- **Long-sleeved UV clothing:** lightweight, UPF 50+ long-sleeved shirts provide better UV protection than sunscreen and do not wash off with sweat. Look for cycling-specific or outdoor brands.

### Heat-Related Illness

- **Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, dizziness. Stop riding, find shade, drink water, cool down.
- **Heat stroke:** confusion, hot dry skin (no sweating), high body temperature. This is a medical emergency — call 000.

**Prevention:**
- Hydrate before and during riding
- Use high assist to minimise effort
- Avoid riding in extreme heat (40 °C+) if possible
- Take breaks in shade if you feel overheated
- Wear light-coloured clothing (reflects heat)

## Tyre Pressure in Heat

Hot air expands. Tyres inflated to the correct pressure on a cool morning will be 5–10 PSI higher by mid-afternoon in 40 °C heat. Over-inflated tyres ride harshly, have less grip, and are more prone to punctures on hot roads.

**Adjustment:** in summer, inflate tyres to the lower end of the recommended range in the morning. The heat will bring them up to the middle of the range during the day.

## Bottom Line

E-bike commuting in Australian summer is manageable and comfortable with the right approach: high motor assist, lightweight clothing, a pannier (not a backpack), shade where possible, and changing at work. The motor does most of the work, so you generate far less heat than on a regular bike. Protect the battery from extreme heat (store indoors, charge after cooling), apply sunscreen, and hydrate. The only days where riding is genuinely inadvisable are extreme heat days above 40 °C — and those are increasingly common, so have a backup plan.
