Cargo e-bikes are replacing cars for school runs across Australian cities. A purpose-built cargo bike carries one, two, or even three children securely, covers 10–15 km on a single charge, and parks at the school gate in 30 seconds. No traffic queue, no parking scramble, no fuel cost. But carrying children on a bicycle demands careful attention to safety.

## Types of Cargo Bikes for Kids

### Long-Tail Cargo Bikes

The rear section of the frame extends behind the seat, providing a long platform for child seats, footpegs, and handrails. One or two children sit behind the rider, either on dedicated child seats or on a padded bench with grab handles.

**Best for:** families with one or two children aged 2–10. The bike handles and rides similarly to a standard bicycle — just longer.

**Example setups:**
- One rear child seat (Thule Yepp Maxi or similar) for a toddler (9 months – 6 years)
- Two children on a padded rear bench with footpegs and grab handles (ages 3–10)
- One child seat + one standing child on a rear deck with handrails (mixed ages)

### Front-Loader (Box) Bikes

A large cargo box sits between the front wheel and the handlebars. Children sit inside the box, below the rider's line of sight. The rider steers via a linkage that turns the front wheel via the box.

**Best for:** families with two or three young children. The box can accommodate a bench seat for two kids plus a baby capsule. Children are visible to the rider (look down into the box) and protected by the box walls.

**Example setups:**
- Two children on a bench seat inside the box (ages 1–8)
- One baby in a capsule/maxi-cosi adapter + one toddler on the bench
- Three children on a wide bench (some boxes fit three small children)

### Compact Cargo Bikes

Shorter than a long-tail, with a reinforced rear section that takes one child seat. A practical compromise between a standard e-bike and a full cargo bike — shorter, lighter, easier to store, but limited to one child.

## Child Seat Options

### Rear-Mounted Child Seats

Bolt to the rear rack or frame. The child faces forward behind the rider.

**Standards:** look for seats that comply with **EN 14344** (European standard for bicycle child seats, widely used in Australia). Key features:
- 3-point or 5-point harness
- Foot guards to prevent feet from entering the wheel spokes
- High back and side protection
- Quick-release mounting system

**Popular models:**
- **Thule Yepp Maxi:** 5-point harness, shock-absorbing seat, fits most racks. For children 9 months to 6 years, up to 22 kg.
- **Hamax Caress:** reclining backrest (child can nap), adjustable footrests. Up to 22 kg.
- **Polisport Guppy Maxi:** budget option with solid safety features. Up to 22 kg.

### Front-Mounted Child Seats

Mount between the handlebars and the rider. The child sits in front, between the rider's arms.

**Best for:** toddlers (9 months – 3 years, up to 15 kg). The child can see ahead, and the rider can see the child. However, front seats affect steering and add weight to the front of the bike.

**Note:** front-mounted seats are less common on e-bikes because the display and controls often occupy the handlebar space. Check compatibility before purchasing.

### Box Bike Seating

Front-loader box bikes have their own seating systems — typically a wooden or plastic bench with 3-point harnesses, built into the box. Some boxes include a rain canopy with clear windows.

## Safety Essentials

### Helmets for Children

**Legally required in every Australian state** for all riders and passengers, regardless of age. Children under 12 months should not ride on a bicycle (their neck muscles cannot support a helmet under impact forces).

Choose a helmet that:
- Meets **AS/NZS 2063** (mandatory in Australia)
- Fits snugly without wobbling
- Has an easy-to-use buckle (the child should not be able to open it)
- Is brightly coloured or has reflective elements

### Harness and Restraints

Every child seat must have a functional harness. **Never ride with an unrestrained child.** A sudden stop at 20 km/h can throw an unsecured child off the bike. Check the harness before every ride — children are remarkably good at loosening straps.

### Spoke Guards

Rear-mounted child seats must have foot guards that prevent the child's feet from reaching the wheel spokes. A foot caught in rotating spokes causes severe injury. If the seat does not have built-in guards, install aftermarket spoke guards on the rear wheel.

### Visibility

Children on cargo bikes sit low — sometimes below the driver's eye line in adjacent cars. Increase visibility with:
- A tall flag or pennant (a fibreglass pole with a bright flag, 1.5 m high, visible above car roofline)
- Reflective stickers on the child seat, box, or frame
- Running lights (front and rear) during every ride, not just at night

### Speed

Ride slower with children on board. Your stopping distance is longer (more weight), your handling is different (altered centre of gravity), and a collision at any speed is more dangerous with a child aboard.

**Practical limit:** keep to 15–20 km/h with children. Use a lower assist level. Prioritise bike paths and quiet streets over main roads.

## Weather Protection

### Rain Canopies

Box bikes can fit a full rain canopy — a clear plastic cover over the box that keeps children dry while letting them see out. Long-tail bikes can use a child seat rain cover (a fitted poncho that covers the seat and child).

### Sun Protection

Australian UV levels are extreme. A canopy with UV-blocking material protects children during summer rides. For open seats, dress children in a hat and sunscreen, and ride in the shade where possible.

### Cold Weather

In winter, children are sitting still while you are exercising — they get cold much faster than you do. Layer children in warm clothing, add a blanket over their legs, and consider handlebar mitts (for you) and a footmuff (for them).

## Practical Tips for School Runs

1. **Plan the route** to use bike paths and quiet streets. Avoid main roads during school-zone hours.
2. **Allow extra time** — you will be slower than a car but faster than walking. A 3 km school run takes 10–12 minutes by cargo bike.
3. **Practice the route without children first** to learn the corners, hills, and intersections.
4. **Park at the school gate** — cargo bikes park in seconds. No circling the block, no double-parking.
5. **Involve the children** — most kids love riding on the cargo bike. Let them help with helmets and talk about what they see. It becomes a positive part of the morning routine.

## Bottom Line

Cargo e-bikes are a safe, practical, and joyful way to do the school run — provided you invest in proper child seats, helmets, harnesses, and visibility equipment. The combination of zero fuel cost, zero parking stress, daily exercise, and quality time with your children makes the cargo e-bike one of the smartest family transport decisions you can make.
