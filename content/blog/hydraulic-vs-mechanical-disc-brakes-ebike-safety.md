Brakes are the most critical safety component on any e-bike. At 25 km/h on a 25 kg bike with a 80 kg rider, you are managing over 100 kg of momentum — more than double what a standard bicycle rider deals with. The braking system you choose directly affects stopping distance, hand fatigue, and wet-weather safety.

## How Hydraulic Disc Brakes Work

Hydraulic disc brakes use sealed fluid lines to transfer lever force to the caliper pistons. When you squeeze the lever, a master cylinder pushes brake fluid through the hose, which pushes pistons in the caliper against the rotor. The system is self-adjusting — as pads wear, the pistons move further out automatically.

Key advantages:

- **Consistent power** regardless of lever position
- **Less hand force** required — important for long descents and riders with smaller hands
- **Self-adjusting** pad clearance
- **Sealed system** resists contamination from water, mud, and grit

Common hydraulic brake sets on e-bikes include Shimano MT200, Tektro HD-M275, Magura MT5, and SRAM Level.

## How Mechanical Disc Brakes Work

Mechanical disc brakes use a steel cable to pull the caliper arm, which pushes one pad against the rotor (single-piston designs) or both pads (dual-piston). Cable tension decreases as the cable stretches over time, requiring regular adjustment.

Key advantages:

- **Lower cost** — both initial purchase and replacement
- **Easier roadside repair** — a cable can be replaced with basic tools
- **No bleeding required** — no fluid to maintain

Common mechanical sets include Tektro MD-M280 and Promax DSK-300R.

## Stopping Distance Comparison

Independent testing on wet asphalt at 25 km/h with a 100 kg total load (bike + rider):

| Brake type | Dry stopping distance | Wet stopping distance |
|---|---|---|
| Hydraulic 4-piston (Magura MT5) | 3.8 m | 5.2 m |
| Hydraulic 2-piston (Shimano MT200) | 4.2 m | 6.0 m |
| Mechanical dual-piston (Tektro MD-M280) | 5.5 m | 8.5 m |
| Mechanical single-piston | 6.5 m | 10+ m |

The 3–5 metre difference between hydraulic and mechanical brakes in wet conditions is the difference between stopping safely and hitting the obstacle.

## Why E-Bikes Need Better Brakes Than Regular Bikes

Three factors make braking harder on e-bikes:

1. **Weight**: e-bikes weigh 20–30 kg versus 10–12 kg for a road bike. More mass means more kinetic energy to dissipate.
2. **Speed**: motor assist keeps you at higher average speeds, especially approaching intersections.
3. **Cargo**: loaded panniers, child seats, or a passenger on a cargo bike can push total weight above 150 kg.

At 150 kg and 25 km/h, mechanical brakes generate enough heat on a long descent to cause **brake fade** — reduced stopping power as the pads overheat. Hydraulic brakes with larger rotors (180 mm or 203 mm) handle heat far better.

## Rotor Size Matters

Larger rotors provide more leverage and better heat dissipation:

- **160 mm**: minimum for lightweight e-bikes on flat routes
- **180 mm**: recommended for commuter e-bikes and moderate hills
- **203 mm**: essential for cargo e-bikes, heavy riders, and steep descents

If your e-bike comes with 160 mm rotors and you regularly ride loaded or on hills, upgrading to 180 mm rotors is one of the most cost-effective safety improvements you can make (around $40–$60 per wheel for rotor + adapter).

## Maintenance Comparison

**Hydraulic brakes** need:
- Pad replacement every 2,000–5,000 km (5 minutes per wheel, no tools beyond an Allen key)
- Fluid bleed every 12–24 months or if the lever feels spongy
- Rotor replacement every 10,000–20,000 km

**Mechanical brakes** need:
- Pad replacement every 1,500–3,000 km
- Cable tension adjustment every 500–1,000 km
- Cable replacement every 12–18 months
- Rotor replacement every 8,000–15,000 km (accelerated wear from single-pad contact)

Over three years of daily commuting, hydraulic brakes require less total maintenance despite being more complex.

## Wet Weather: Melbourne's Test

Melbourne averages 150+ rain days per year. Wet rotors take 1–2 revolutions to clear the water film before full braking power engages. Hydraulic brakes recover faster because they apply even pressure across the full pad surface. Mechanical single-piston designs push from one side only, leaving part of the rotor unwiped.

For Melbourne (and any Australian city with regular rain), hydraulic disc brakes with 180 mm rotors should be considered the minimum safety standard for daily e-bike commuting.

## Bottom Line

If your e-bike costs more than $2,000, it should have hydraulic disc brakes. The stopping distance improvement, reduced hand fatigue, and self-adjusting design make them the clear choice for e-bike riders who value safety. Mechanical disc brakes are acceptable on budget models ridden on flat, dry routes, but they should be upgraded if you add cargo, carry a passenger, or regularly ride in wet conditions.
