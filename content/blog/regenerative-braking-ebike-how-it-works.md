Regenerative braking captures energy during braking and feeds it back into the battery. It sounds like free energy — and it is, in a sense. But on an e-bike, the reality is more modest than the marketing suggests.

## How It Works

When you brake (or coast downhill), the motor runs in reverse — acting as a generator instead of a motor. The kinetic energy of the moving bike is converted into electrical energy and stored in the battery.

**In an electric car,** regenerative braking recovers 15–30 % of the energy used in city driving. Cars are heavy (1,500–2,500 kg), travel at high speeds, and brake frequently and hard. There is a lot of kinetic energy to recover.

**On an e-bike,** the numbers are much smaller. A rider + bike weighs 90–120 kg total, travels at 15–25 km/h, and brakes gently. The kinetic energy available at any given braking event is tiny — roughly 1/50th of what a car generates.

## How Much Energy Does It Actually Recover?

**Realistic recovery on an e-bike: 5–10 % of total battery capacity per ride.**

If your battery is 500 Wh and your ride uses 200 Wh, regenerative braking might recover 10–20 Wh — extending your range by 2–5 km on a 40 km ride. Useful, but not transformative.

**Factors that increase recovery:**
- Long, steep descents (more energy to capture)
- Frequent braking (stop-start city traffic)
- Heavier rider + cargo (more kinetic energy)
- Higher speeds before braking

**Factors that decrease recovery:**
- Flat terrain (no gravitational energy to recover)
- Smooth, constant-speed riding (no braking events)
- Light rider (less kinetic energy)
- Short braking distances (not enough time to generate significant current)

## Which Motors Support Regenerative Braking?

### Hub Motors — Yes (Direct Drive Only)

**Direct-drive hub motors** are the most common motors with regenerative braking. Because the motor is directly connected to the wheel (no gears, no freewheel), the motor spins whenever the wheel spins — making it naturally suited to regen.

- The motor coasts as a generator when you stop pedalling or apply the brakes
- Regen can be activated by brake levers (brake sensors trigger regen) or by a throttle return-to-zero signal
- Some controllers offer adjustable regen levels (light, medium, strong)

**Geared hub motors** typically do NOT support regen. They have an internal freewheel that disconnects the motor from the wheel when coasting — meaning the motor does not spin during braking and cannot generate electricity.

### Mid-Drive Motors — Almost Never

Mid-drive motors (Bosch, Shimano, Bafang) do not use regenerative braking. The motor drives the chain, and a freewheel between the chainring and the rear wheel disconnects the motor when coasting. Even if the motor could generate electricity, the chain is not engaged during braking.

**Exception:** some custom mid-drive setups and prototype systems claim regen capability, but none of the major mid-drive manufacturers (Bosch, Shimano, Yamaha, Bafang) offer it in production e-bikes.

## The Feel of Regenerative Braking

Regenerative braking changes how the bike feels when coasting.

### Drag
When regen is active, the motor creates resistance — like riding into a headwind or dragging a brake. This slows the bike without touching the brake levers.

- **Light regen:** barely noticeable. A gentle deceleration when you stop pedalling. Feels natural.
- **Medium regen:** noticeable drag. The bike slows down meaningfully when you stop pedalling. Takes some adjustment.
- **Strong regen:** significant drag. The bike decelerates quickly without brakes. Some riders find this uncomfortable or unsafe (following riders may not expect you to slow down without visible braking).

### One-Pedal Riding
At strong regen settings, you can ride using mostly the pedals and throttle, with the brakes reserved for emergencies. Pedal to go, stop pedalling to slow down. This is common on electric motorcycles and electric cars, but feels unusual on a bicycle.

**Safety note:** regenerative braking does NOT activate your brake light (if fitted). Following traffic sees no visual signal that you are slowing down. Always use your actual brakes for significant deceleration.

## Pros and Cons

### Advantages
- **Free range extension** (small but real — 5–10 %)
- **Reduced brake wear** (regen does some of the braking work, so pads last longer)
- **Controlled descents** (regen slows you on long downhills without overheating brake pads)
- **Simpler motor design** (direct-drive hub motors have no gears to wear out)

### Disadvantages
- **Marginal energy recovery** (not enough to meaningfully change your range planning)
- **Coasting drag** (you cannot coast freely — the motor always creates some resistance)
- **Heavier motors** (direct-drive hub motors with regen are typically 1–2 kg heavier than geared hub motors)
- **No brake light activation** (safety concern)
- **Limited to hub motors** (if you want a mid-drive for hills, you cannot have regen)
- **Marketing overstatement** (some brands imply regen significantly extends range — it rarely adds more than 10 %)

## When Regenerative Braking Is Worth It

**Good use cases:**
- **Hilly commutes with long descents:** a 2 km descent can recover meaningful energy
- **Stop-start city riding:** frequent braking events add up
- **Delivery riders:** hundreds of braking events per shift, heavy loads, long shifts — regen adds up over a full day
- **Riders who want reduced brake maintenance:** regen reduces pad wear, which matters for heavy riders and frequent brakers

**Not worth prioritising:**
- **Flat terrain commuters:** minimal braking, minimal recovery
- **Riders who want a mid-drive motor:** regen is not available on mid-drives
- **Riders who prioritise light weight:** direct-drive hub motors are the heaviest motor type
- **Riders making a purchase decision:** do not choose a bike primarily for regen — choose it for the right motor, battery, and fit, and treat regen as a bonus if it comes with your motor type

## Bottom Line

Regenerative braking on e-bikes is real but modest — expect 5–10 % range extension, not the 20–30 % that electric cars achieve. It is a nice bonus on a direct-drive hub motor, not a reason to choose one motor type over another. If your ideal bike happens to have regen, enjoy the free kilometres. If it does not, you are not missing much.
