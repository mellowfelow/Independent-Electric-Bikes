Your first e-bike commute is a test run. Get it right and you will never want to drive again. Get it wrong — wrong route, wrong clothes, no lock plan — and the bike sits in the garage. Here is a checklist to make the first ride smooth.

## Step 1: Plan the Route (Not the Fastest — the Safest)

Do not simply follow the car route on a bike. Car-optimal routes use main roads with high speed limits, no bike lanes, and aggressive traffic. Bike-optimal routes use:

- **Bike paths and shared paths:** separated from traffic, lower stress, usually scenic
- **Quiet residential streets:** 40 km/h or 50 km/h zones with low traffic volume
- **Bike lanes:** dedicated or painted lanes on main roads (better than nothing, but separated paths are safer)

### Tools for Route Planning

- **Google Maps (cycling mode):** shows bike-friendly routes, elevation profiles, and estimated ride times. It knows about bike paths that do not appear in driving directions.
- **Strava Route Builder:** uses aggregated rider data to find popular cycling routes. If many cyclists use a road, it is probably safe and pleasant.
- **Your local council cycling map:** most Australian councils publish cycling network maps showing bike paths, shared paths, and recommended on-road routes.

### Test the Route on a Weekend

Ride the full route on a quiet weekend morning — no time pressure, light traffic. Note:
- Where you feel unsafe (busy intersections, no bike lane, fast traffic)
- Where the surface is rough (potholes, tram tracks, gravel)
- Where you can stop safely (traffic lights, pedestrian crossings)
- Where to park and lock at both ends
- How long it actually takes (add 10 % for traffic lights and stops)

## Step 2: Time the Ride

Your e-bike commute will typically take:
- **5 km:** 15–20 minutes
- **10 km:** 25–35 minutes
- **15 km:** 35–50 minutes
- **20 km:** 45–65 minutes

These times assume 20–25 km/h average speed including stops. Hills, headwinds, and traffic lights add time. Factor in 5 minutes for locking up and 5 minutes for changing clothes (if needed) at each end.

## Step 3: What to Wear

### If Your Commute Is Under 10 km

Ride in your work clothes (with a few adjustments):
- Trouser clips or roll your right pant leg to keep it out of the chain
- Flat or low-heeled shoes that grip the pedals
- A light jacket for wind — remove it on arrival

E-bikes reduce sweat compared to regular bikes because the motor does 30–50 % of the work. On Eco or Normal assist, a 10 km flat commute barely raises a sweat.

### If Your Commute Is Over 10 km or Hilly

Ride in cycling-specific or athletic clothing and change at work:
- Moisture-wicking top
- Cycling shorts or athletic pants
- Cycling shoes or trainers
- Pack work clothes in a pannier (not a backpack — panniers keep clothes wrinkle-free and your back dry)

### Rain Gear (Keep in Your Bag)

A lightweight packable rain jacket, waterproof pants, and shoe covers. Melbourne riders should carry rain gear on every ride — the weather changes fast.

## Step 4: What to Carry

### In Your Panniers or Bag

- Work clothes (if changing)
- Laptop and charger
- Lunch
- Water bottle
- Rain jacket
- Phone, wallet, keys

### On the Bike

- Lock (mounted on the frame or in a pannier)
- Lights (front and rear, charged)
- Spare tube, tyre levers, mini pump
- Multi-tool

### On Your Body

- Helmet (legally required)
- Sunglasses or clear glasses (eye protection from wind and debris)

## Step 5: Lock Strategy

Before your first commute, answer: **where will I lock the bike and with what?**

- Is there secure bike parking at your workplace? (Cage, indoor room, covered stand)
- If not, is there a solid object near your entrance where you can lock?
- Do you have a quality U-lock and cable? (See our lock guide)
- Can you remove the battery and take it inside? (Reduces theft incentive)

If your workplace has no bike parking, ask. Many employers will install a bike rack or designate indoor storage if someone asks — especially if you frame it as supporting employee health and reducing parking demand.

## Step 6: Charge and Range Check

- **Charge the battery to 100 %** the night before your first commute
- **Check that your round-trip distance is within 60 % of your rated range.** You do not want to run the battery flat on day one.
- **Identify a charging option at work** — a standard wall outlet near your desk or in the bike storage area. Most e-bike chargers draw less than 250 W (less than a phone charger and laptop combined).

## Step 7: The First Ride

- **Leave 15 minutes early.** No time pressure on the first commute.
- **Use moderate assist (Tour/Normal).** Do not blow through the battery on Turbo; do not arrive exhausted on Eco. Find the middle.
- **Ride predictably.** Signal turns, hold your line, make eye contact with drivers at intersections.
- **Take the planned route.** Do not improvise on day one — stick to the tested path.

## Step 8: At Work

- Lock the bike securely
- Remove the battery if parking is outdoors (or if theft is a concern)
- Change clothes if needed
- Plug in the charger if you have access to an outlet

## Step 9: After the First Week

By the end of week one, you will know:
- Whether the route works or needs adjustment
- What assist level suits your fitness and the terrain
- Whether you arrive sweaty (and need to change) or fresh (and can ride in work clothes)
- How much battery you use per commute (and whether you need to charge at work)

Most new e-bike commuters report that by day three, the routine feels completely natural — and by day five, driving feels like a waste of time.

## Bottom Line

Planning the first commute takes 30 minutes. Riding it takes 20–40 minutes. The satisfaction of arriving at work having ridden past every traffic jam lasts all day. Start with one day per week if you are unsure, and build from there. Most riders who start with one day quickly move to three, then five.
