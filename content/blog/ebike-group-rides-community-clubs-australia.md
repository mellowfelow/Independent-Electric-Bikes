E-bike group rides are growing across Australia. They are social, they cover more ground than walking groups, and the motor equalises fitness levels — a 60-year-old on a step-through can ride alongside a 25-year-old on a mountain bike without either holding the other back.

## Finding Group Rides

### Local Bike Shops
Many bike shops organise regular group rides — often weekly or monthly. E-bike-specific rides are increasingly common, especially at shops that sell e-bikes. Ask your local shop.

### Cycling Clubs
Traditional cycling clubs have historically been road-bike-focused, but many now welcome e-bikes:
- **Bicycle Network** (national): organises events and connects riders across Australia
- **Amy Gillett Foundation rides:** community safety-focused events
- **Local cycling clubs:** search your state's cycling body for affiliated clubs

Some clubs have e-bike-specific groups or social ride categories that accommodate different speeds and fitness levels.

### Online Communities
- **Facebook groups:** search "[your city] e-bike riders" or "[your city] electric bike group." Most Australian cities have active groups.
- **Strava clubs:** Strava has e-bike-specific clubs and segments. Join a local club and find riders on similar routes.
- **Meetup.com:** search for e-bike or cycling meetup groups in your area.

### Organised Events
- **Around the Bay (Melbourne):** one of Australia's largest cycling events. E-bikes are welcome.
- **MS Melbourne Cycle and other charity rides:** most charity rides welcome e-bikes.
- **Local council rides:** some councils organise free community rides, including e-bike events. Check your council's events calendar.

## Organising Your Own Group Ride

### Planning the Route
1. **Distance:** 20–40 km is ideal for a social group ride on e-bikes. Short enough that battery is not a concern for anyone, long enough to feel like an adventure.
2. **Terrain:** choose flat to moderately hilly routes. Steep climbs split the group — some riders sprint ahead while others grind slowly. The motor reduces this problem but does not eliminate it.
3. **Surface:** sealed bike paths and quiet back roads are best. Avoid busy main roads and rough trails unless the group is experienced.
4. **Coffee stop:** every group ride needs a cafe stop. Plan it at the halfway point. The stop regroups everyone, refuels energy, and makes the ride social.
5. **Bail-out points:** plan escape routes where riders can leave early if they need to (flat tyre, fatigue, time constraints, or battery running low).

### Group Size
- **3–8 riders:** manageable, easy to communicate, stays together naturally
- **8–15 riders:** needs a ride leader and a sweep rider (someone at the back to make sure nobody is left behind)
- **15+:** consider splitting into subgroups with staggered starts

### Communication
- **Pre-ride briefing:** route overview, distance, elevation, cafe stop location, and expected pace
- **Hand signals:** point out hazards (potholes, glass, parked cars), signal turns, signal stopping
- **Ride leader and sweep:** the leader sets the pace at the front; the sweep stays at the back and communicates with the leader (phone or two-way radio for large groups)

### Pace
The biggest challenge in group riding is pace management. On regular bikes, fitness determines pace and slower riders are dropped. On e-bikes, the motor reduces the gap — but it does not eliminate it, because riders use different assist levels and have different natural cadences.

**Solutions:**
- **Set a maximum speed:** 20–22 km/h is social and comfortable for most riders. Faster riders adjust their assist down.
- **Regroup at turns and hills:** the lead rider waits at every turn and at the top of every climb for the group to regroup.
- **No-drop policy:** state clearly that no rider will be left behind. This is the defining rule of a social ride.

## Etiquette

### Shared Paths
- **Single file** when passing pedestrians or oncoming cyclists
- **Bell or call out** before passing ("passing on your right")
- **Slow down** around pedestrians, children, and dogs
- **Do not block the path** — ride no more than two abreast and leave room for others to pass

### Roads
- **Ride predictably** — signal turns, hold your line, do not weave
- **Two abreast maximum** (legal in most states, but switch to single file when cars are passing)
- **Do not run red lights or stop signs** — the group follows road rules
- **Thank drivers who wait** — a wave or nod builds goodwill

### Within the Group
- **Do not half-wheel** (riding half a wheel ahead of the person beside you — it is unconsciously competitive and annoying)
- **Warn about hazards** (call out "hole," "glass," "car back")
- **Do not surge ahead on climbs** just because your motor is more powerful — wait at the top
- **Carry your own tools and spares** — do not rely on others to fix your puncture

## Benefits of Group Riding

### Safety
A group of riders is more visible to drivers than a solo rider. Drivers give more room and pay more attention to a group.

### Motivation
Committing to a regular group ride gives you a reason to ride even when you do not feel like it. Social accountability beats willpower.

### Route Discovery
Group members share routes you would never find on your own — quiet streets, hidden bike paths, scenic detours, the best cafe stops.

### Mechanical Help
If you get a flat, someone in the group has a pump. If your chain breaks, someone has a chain tool. Group knowledge solves problems that would strand a solo rider.

### Social Connection
Cycling is often solitary. Group rides turn exercise into socialising. Many group riders report that the social aspect is more valuable than the cycling itself — especially for retirees, remote workers, and people new to an area.

## E-Bike-Specific Considerations

### Battery Management
On a group ride, the rider with the smallest battery or the most worn battery sets the range limit. Before the ride, check:
- What is the shortest battery range in the group?
- Does the route stay within that range?
- Is there a charging point on the route (cafe with a power outlet)?

### Speed Equalisation
E-bikes make group riding more inclusive — the motor compensates for fitness differences. But riders on more powerful bikes (80+ Nm mid-drive) may need to ride in a lower assist mode to match riders on less powerful bikes (40 Nm hub motor). Discuss this before the ride.

### Weight and Handling
E-bikes are heavier than regular bikes. In a group, this means:
- Longer stopping distances (leave more space between riders)
- More momentum in corners (do not cut inside other riders)
- Harder to manoeuvre at low speed (allow more room at cafe stops and bike racks)

## Bottom Line

E-bike group rides are one of the best ways to enjoy cycling — social, safe, inclusive, and accessible regardless of fitness level. Find a group through your local bike shop, cycling club, or Facebook community, or start your own with 3–5 friends. Set a comfortable pace (20–22 km/h), include a cafe stop, adopt a no-drop policy, and ride regularly. The consistency of a weekly group ride delivers more health and social benefits than any solo riding routine.
