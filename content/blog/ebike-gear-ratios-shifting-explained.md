Gears on an e-bike work the same way as gears on a regular bike — but the motor changes when and how you use them. Understanding gear ratios helps you ride more efficiently, extend your battery range, and reduce drivetrain wear.

## What Gears Do

Gears change the ratio between how fast you pedal (cadence) and how fast the wheel turns. A low gear makes pedalling easy but slow — good for climbing. A high gear makes pedalling hard but fast — good for cruising on flat ground.

**Without gears:** you have one gear ratio. On flat ground it might feel fine, but on a hill you grind slowly, and on a descent you spin uselessly.

**With gears:** you shift to match the terrain. Easy gear for hills, hard gear for flats and descents. Your legs work at a comfortable effort level regardless of the terrain.

## Gear Ratio Basics

The gear ratio is the number of teeth on the front chainring divided by the number of teeth on the rear cog.

**Example:**
- Front chainring: 42 teeth
- Rear cog: 21 teeth
- Gear ratio: 42/21 = 2.0

This means for every pedal revolution, the rear wheel turns 2.0 times. A higher ratio = harder to pedal but faster. A lower ratio = easier to pedal but slower.

### Gear Range

The gear range is the difference between the lowest and highest gear ratios. A wider range means more versatility — you can climb steeper hills and cruise faster on flats.

**E-bike example (1× drivetrain, 42T chainring, 11-51T cassette):**
- Lowest gear: 42/51 = 0.82 (very easy, for steep climbs)
- Highest gear: 42/11 = 3.82 (very hard, for fast flats and descents)
- Gear range: 3.82 / 0.82 = 4.66 (a 466 % range — wide enough for almost any terrain)

## Derailleur vs Internal Hub Gears

### Derailleur (External Gears)

The chain moves between different-sized cogs on the rear cassette (and sometimes between chainrings at the front). The standard system on most bikes.

**E-bike configuration:** most modern e-bikes use a 1× (single front chainring) drivetrain with a wide-range rear cassette (10–12 speeds). This simplifies the system (no front derailleur, no chain drop) while providing adequate gear range.

**Common cassette sizes:**
- 11-42T (10-speed): adequate for moderate hills
- 11-46T (11-speed): good for hilly terrain
- 11-51T or 11-52T (12-speed SRAM/Shimano): excellent range for steep hills and fast flats

**Advantages:**
- Light and efficient (minimal power loss)
- Wide gear range
- Easy to service and replace
- Cheap replacement parts

**Disadvantages:**
- Cannot shift while stationary (you must be pedalling to shift)
- Exposed to weather, dirt, and damage
- Chain and cassette wear out faster on e-bikes (motor torque)
- Shifting under heavy load (motor + pedal force) can crunch the chain

### Internal Hub Gears

The gears are enclosed inside the rear hub. You shift by moving an internal mechanism — no derailleur, no exposed cassette.

**Common systems:**
- **Shimano Nexus 3/5/7/8-speed:** basic internal hubs. Narrower range than a derailleur but adequate for flat to moderate terrain.
- **Shimano Alfine 8/11-speed:** wider range, smoother shifting. Good for hilly commuting.
- **Rohloff Speedhub 14-speed:** the gold standard. 526 % gear range, virtually indestructible, shift under load. Expensive ($1,500+ for the hub alone).
- **Enviolo (continuously variable):** no discrete gears. You twist a grip to infinitely vary the ratio within the hub's range. Smooth, intuitive, silent. Popular on premium commuter e-bikes.

**Advantages:**
- Shift while stationary (useful for stop-start city riding — shift to a low gear at a red light before you start pedalling)
- Enclosed from weather and dirt (low maintenance)
- No chain drop or derailleur damage
- Long service life (10,000+ km between services for Rohloff and Enviolo)

**Disadvantages:**
- Heavier than derailleur (1–2 kg more)
- Less efficient (1–5 % power loss inside the hub)
- Narrower gear range on cheaper hubs (Nexus 3/5)
- Expensive to replace if damaged
- Cannot be serviced at every bike shop (specialist knowledge required for Rohloff and Enviolo)

## How Gears Interact with the Motor

### Mid-Drive Motors

On a mid-drive e-bike, the motor drives the chain — so it uses the gears. This is critical:

- **Low gear + motor = maximum climbing force.** The motor's torque is multiplied by the gear ratio. In a low gear (42/51), the motor effectively delivers ~2× its crank torque at the wheel.
- **High gear + motor = maximum speed.** In a high gear, the motor spins faster relative to the wheel — reaching the 25 km/h assist cutoff sooner.
- **Shifting matters.** Using the wrong gear wastes motor energy and accelerates chain wear. The motor is most efficient at a moderate cadence (60–80 RPM) — shift to maintain this range.

**Mid-drive shifting rules:**
1. Shift to a lower gear before a hill (not during the climb under load)
2. Ease off the pedals momentarily when shifting (reduces chain stress)
3. Do not cross-chain (big-big or small-small if you have a 2× drivetrain)
4. Shift down at stops so you start in an easy gear

### Hub Motors

On a hub motor e-bike, the motor drives the wheel directly — independently of the gears. The gears only affect your pedal input.

- **Shifting still matters for your comfort** — you want to pedal at a comfortable cadence — but the motor does not benefit from the gears.
- **You can shift under load** without worrying about motor torque crunching the chain (only your leg force goes through the chain).
- **The motor works equally hard in any gear** — it does not get a mechanical advantage from low gears like a mid-drive does.

## Cadence: The Optimal Pedalling Speed

Cadence is how fast you pedal, measured in revolutions per minute (RPM). Most e-bike motor systems are optimised for a cadence of 60–80 RPM.

**Why cadence matters:**
- **Below 50 RPM:** you are grinding in too high a gear. The motor works harder, the chain is under more stress, and your knees take more force. Shift to a lower gear.
- **60–80 RPM:** the sweet spot. The motor operates efficiently, the chain is under moderate stress, and your pedalling is comfortable.
- **Above 90 RPM:** you are spinning in too low a gear. The motor cannot add much power because the cadence sensor is already at maximum, and you are not contributing much force. Shift to a higher gear.

**Tip:** most e-bike displays show cadence. If yours does not, count pedal revolutions for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. Aim for 15–20 revolutions in 15 seconds (60–80 RPM).

## Gear Recommendations by Use Case

| Use case | Recommended gearing |
|---|---|
| Flat city commuting | 7–8 speed internal hub or 1×10 derailleur |
| Hilly city commuting | 11-speed Alfine, Enviolo, or 1×12 derailleur (11-51T) |
| Mountain / trail | 1×12 derailleur (11-51T or 11-52T) |
| Touring / loaded riding | Rohloff 14-speed or 1×12 derailleur |
| Minimal maintenance priority | Enviolo CVT or Shimano Nexus/Alfine |

## Bottom Line

Gears on an e-bike serve the same purpose as on a regular bike — keep your legs pedalling at a comfortable speed regardless of terrain. On a mid-drive e-bike, correct gear use extends range, reduces chain wear, and improves hill performance. On a hub motor e-bike, gears primarily affect your comfort. For most commuters, a 1×10 or 1×11 derailleur with a wide-range cassette is the best balance of range, simplicity, and cost. For low-maintenance commuting, an internal hub (Enviolo or Alfine) is worth the premium.
