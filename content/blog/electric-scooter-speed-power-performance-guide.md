## E-Scooter Speed and Power Ratings Explained: Watts, Voltage, and Real-World Performance

E-scooter spec sheets are full of numbers: 500W motor, 48V battery, 25 km/h top speed, 600W peak power. But what do these figures actually mean for your riding experience? Understanding the relationship between watts, voltage, amperage, and real-world performance helps you cut through marketing claims and choose a scooter that performs as you need it to. This guide explains the key specifications in practical terms.

## Watts: The Power Rating

Watts (W) measure the rate at which the motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy. It is the most commonly quoted motor specification and the one most often misunderstood.

### Continuous vs Peak Power

Every e-scooter motor has two power ratings:

**Continuous (rated) power** is the amount of power the motor can sustain indefinitely without overheating. A motor rated at 350W continuous can produce 350W of mechanical output all day.

**Peak power** is the maximum output the motor can produce for short bursts, typically during acceleration or hill climbing. A motor rated at 350W continuous might have a 700W peak rating. Peak power cannot be sustained — the motor will overheat if asked to produce peak power continuously.

Marketing materials often highlight peak power because the number is bigger. A scooter advertised as "700W" might only sustain 350W. Check whether the quoted figure is continuous or peak. Continuous power is the more honest indicator of performance.

### What Different Power Levels Mean in Practice

**250W continuous:** Adequate for flat terrain and lighter riders (under 70 kg). Struggles on hills and with heavier riders. Typical of budget scooters.

**350W continuous:** Handles moderate inclines and riders up to 85 kg. The minimum for practical commuting on anything other than perfectly flat ground.

**500W continuous:** Competent all-round performance. Handles most urban hills, riders up to 100 kg, and maintains speed into headwinds. The sweet spot for commuter scooters.

**750W+ continuous:** Strong performance for hilly areas and heavier riders. Often exceeds the legal power limits in some Australian jurisdictions, so check local regulations.

**1000W+ continuous (dual motor):** Performance-oriented. Rapid acceleration and steep hill climbing. Primarily for private property use or jurisdictions that permit higher-power scooters.

### Power and Australian Law

Australian e-scooter regulations vary by state. Some jurisdictions specify a maximum motor power (commonly 500W or 600W), while others focus on maximum speed (25 km/h). A scooter that is speed-limited to 25 km/h but has a 1000W motor may or may not be compliant depending on your state's specific wording. Check whether your jurisdiction regulates power, speed, or both.

## Voltage: The Battery's Pressure

Voltage (V) is the electrical pressure pushing current through the system. E-scooter batteries typically range from 24V to 72V.

Higher voltage systems deliver more power to the motor, all else being equal. A 48V system can push a motor harder than a 36V system of the same capacity.

### Common Voltage Levels

**24V:** Entry-level scooters. Limited speed and hill climbing.

**36V:** Standard for mid-range commuter scooters. Adequate performance for flat to moderate terrain.

**48V:** The common choice for higher-performance commuter and enthusiast scooters. Good hill climbing and speed maintenance.

**52V to 72V:** Performance and long-range scooters. More power delivery but also larger, heavier batteries.

Voltage also affects range. A 48V 15Ah battery stores 720Wh (watt-hours) of energy, while a 36V 15Ah battery stores only 540Wh. The higher-voltage battery provides 33 percent more energy storage at the same amp-hour rating.

## Amp-Hours and Watt-Hours: Energy Storage

**Amp-hours (Ah)** measure battery capacity — how much current the battery can supply over time. A 15Ah battery can theoretically supply 15 amps for one hour, or 7.5 amps for two hours.

**Watt-hours (Wh)** are the more useful metric because they account for voltage. Wh = V x Ah. A 48V 15Ah battery stores 720Wh. A 36V 20Ah battery stores 720Wh. Both contain the same amount of energy despite different amp-hour ratings.

When comparing scooter range, compare watt-hours, not amp-hours. As a rough guide, most e-scooters consume 15 to 25Wh per kilometre in real-world riding. A 500Wh battery should deliver 20 to 33 km of real-world range depending on conditions.

## Torque: The Climbing Force

Torque is the rotational force the motor produces. It determines how well the scooter accelerates from a stop and climbs hills. Unfortunately, many e-scooter manufacturers do not publish torque figures.

As a proxy, the combination of motor power and wheel size gives a rough indication of torque. Higher wattage generally means more torque. Smaller wheels amplify available torque at the expense of top speed (like a low gear on a bicycle). This is why scooters with 8-inch wheels can feel punchier at low speeds than 10-inch wheel scooters with the same motor.

Hub motor scooters (where the motor is inside the wheel) have a fixed gear ratio. There is no transmission to shift. This means the motor must produce usable torque across the entire speed range, from standstill to top speed. It is a compromise — hub motors are not as efficient at very low or very high speeds as they are in the middle of their operating range.

## Controller: The Brain

The motor controller regulates how much power flows from the battery to the motor. It interprets your throttle input and manages power delivery.

A 500W motor with a controller limited to 15A on a 36V system can only deliver 540W (V x A). The same motor with a 20A controller delivers 720W. The controller is often the limiting factor in scooter performance, not the motor itself.

Speed modes (eco, standard, sport) are controller settings that limit the maximum current flow. Eco mode might limit current to 10A, while sport mode allows 20A. This is why the same scooter feels dramatically different between modes.

## Real-World Performance vs Spec Sheets

Spec sheet numbers describe theoretical maximums under ideal conditions. Real-world performance is always lower. Here is why:

**Top speed claims** are measured on flat ground with a lightweight rider. A 25 km/h rated scooter ridden by a 90 kg rider on a slight incline may only manage 20 km/h.

**Hill climbing claims** (when provided) are tested at full battery. As the battery depletes, voltage drops and available power decreases. A scooter that climbs a 15-degree hill easily at full charge may struggle at 30 percent battery.

**Acceleration** slows with battery depletion for the same reason. The scooter feels noticeably slower in the last 20 percent of battery life.

**Range claims** are tested under best-case conditions. Expect 60 to 75 percent of advertised range in typical Australian riding conditions.

## How These Numbers Work Together

Understanding how watts, volts, and amps interact helps you evaluate scooters:

**Power (W) = Voltage (V) x Current (A)**

A 48V scooter drawing 15A produces 720W. If the motor is rated at 500W continuous, that 720W represents a level above continuous rating — the motor is working hard but within its peak capability.

**Energy (Wh) = Voltage (V) x Capacity (Ah)**

A 48V 13Ah battery holds 624Wh. At a consumption rate of 20Wh/km, that battery provides roughly 31 km of range.

**Speed** depends on motor power, rider weight, terrain, wind, and tyre pressure. Two scooters with identical motors will perform differently depending on everything else in the system.

## Practical Buying Advice

When evaluating e-scooter performance specs:

1. **Look at continuous power, not peak.** Peak power sounds impressive but tells you less about daily riding performance.

2. **Compare battery capacity in watt-hours.** This is the fairest comparison of range potential between scooters.

3. **Consider your weight.** If you are above 85 kg, add 20 to 30 percent to the minimum power you think you need. Performance claims are typically tested with 70 kg riders.

4. **Think about hills.** Flat-terrain riders can get away with less power. Hilly commutes demand more watts and higher voltage for consistent performance.

5. **Check legal limits.** In your state, ensure the scooter's continuous power rating and maximum speed comply with regulations. A scooter that exceeds legal limits is a liability, not an asset.

6. **Test ride if possible.** Numbers on a spec sheet cannot tell you how a scooter feels. The same 500W motor can feel smooth and responsive on one scooter and jerky on another, depending on the controller software and throttle mapping.

## The Spec That Matters Most

If you can only pay attention to one number, make it watt-hours. Battery capacity determines range, and range determines whether your scooter is practical daily transport or something that leaves you stranded mid-commute. All the power in the world means nothing if the battery runs flat before you get where you are going.

For Australian commuters, a scooter with 400 to 700Wh battery capacity, 350 to 500W continuous motor power, and a 36V or 48V system covers the vast majority of urban commuting needs within legal limits. Anything above that is either solving a specific problem (steep hills, heavy rider, long distance) or entering performance territory that cannot be legally used on public roads.
