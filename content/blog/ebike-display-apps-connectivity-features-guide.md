The display on your e-bike handlebar is not just a speedometer — it is the interface between you and the motor, the battery, and increasingly, a connected app ecosystem. Here is what modern e-bike displays show, what the apps do, and how much of it actually matters.

## What the Display Shows

Every e-bike display shows the basics:

- **Speed:** current speed in km/h
- **Battery level:** percentage or bar graph showing remaining charge
- **Assist level:** which pedal-assist mode is active (Eco, Normal, Sport, Turbo or 1–5)
- **Odometer:** total distance ridden since the bike was new
- **Trip distance:** distance since the last reset

Better displays also show:

- **Range estimate:** how many kilometres remain at the current assist level and speed. This number is an estimate — it changes as terrain, wind, and assist level change.
- **Motor power output:** real-time watts the motor is drawing
- **Cadence:** your pedalling speed in RPM
- **Clock**
- **Average speed**
- **Maximum speed**
- **Calories burned (estimated)**
- **Battery voltage**

## Display Types

### Basic LED Displays

Simple panel with LED indicators for battery level and assist mode. No screen, no data, just lights. Found on budget e-bikes.

**Pros:** cheap, weather-resistant, cannot break what is not there.
**Cons:** no speed, no range, no trip data. You are riding blind.

### Monochrome LCD Displays

A small LCD screen (typically 2–3 inches) showing speed, battery, assist level, and basic trip data. The standard on mid-range e-bikes.

**Pros:** readable in direct sunlight, low power consumption, adequate information.
**Cons:** small text, limited data, no backlight on some models (unreadable at night).

### Colour TFT Displays

A high-resolution colour screen (3–4 inches) with detailed graphics, maps (on some models), and multiple data screens. Found on premium e-bikes.

**Pros:** easy to read, attractive, can show navigation (on connected models), customisable screens.
**Cons:** harder to read in direct sunlight (glare), higher power consumption, more expensive to replace if damaged.

### Smartphone Integration (No Built-In Display)

Some e-bikes skip the dedicated display entirely and use your phone as the display via Bluetooth. A handlebar phone mount holds your phone, and the bike's app shows speed, battery, assist level, and navigation.

**Pros:** larger screen, better maps, software updates improve the interface over time, no dedicated display cost.
**Cons:** phone battery drain, screen visibility in sunlight depends on your phone, rain exposure risk, phone mount vibration can damage phone cameras.

## App Ecosystems

### Bosch eBike Flow

Bosch's app connects to the Bosch smart system (2022+ models). Features:
- Real-time ride data synced from the bike
- Customisable assist profiles (adjust motor response, maximum speed, and torque curves)
- Navigation with turn-by-turn directions
- Ride history and statistics
- Firmware updates for the motor and display
- Theft alarm and location tracking (on models with ConnectModule)

### Shimano E-TUBE

Shimano's app for STEPS motor systems. Features:
- Customise assist levels (adjust power delivery per level)
- Firmware updates
- Error diagnostics
- Ride logging
- Less feature-rich than Bosch but reliable

### Specialized Mission Control

For Specialized e-bikes with the SL or Brose motor. Features:
- Real-time tuning of assist levels
- Range estimator with route elevation data
- Ride recording
- Firmware updates
- Motor diagnostics

### Bafang Go

Bafang's app for bikes using Bafang motors (including aftermarket conversions). Features:
- Basic ride data
- Assist level adjustment
- Firmware updates (limited)
- Less polished than Bosch or Shimano apps

### Generic / No-Brand Apps

Budget e-bikes often pair with generic apps via Bluetooth. Quality varies dramatically:
- Some are functional but basic (speed, battery, trip)
- Some are poorly translated, buggy, and abandoned
- Some collect data you may not want to share

**Recommendation:** do not choose a bike based on its app. Apps are a bonus, not a buying criterion. The motor, battery, and frame matter more.

## What Actually Matters

After the novelty wears off (about two weeks), most riders use the display for three things:

1. **Battery percentage** — to know whether they will make it home
2. **Current speed** — to stay within the speed limit on shared paths
3. **Assist level** — to adjust effort for hills or headwinds

Everything else — cadence, calories, average speed, motor power — is interesting but not essential. Do not pay a premium for a fancy display if the motor and battery are adequate. A $50 monochrome LCD that shows speed, battery, and assist level serves 95 % of riders perfectly.

## Anti-Theft and Tracking

Some display/app combinations include:
- **Lock mode:** disables the motor when you walk away. The bike can still be pedalled (it is a bicycle, after all) but without motor assist, a 25 kg e-bike is not much fun to ride away on.
- **Alarm:** vibration sensor triggers a loud alarm and/or a phone notification. Useful in café stops but not a substitute for a physical lock.
- **GPS tracking:** real-time location via the app if the bike is moved. Requires a cellular connection (SIM card or built-in module). The best factory-integrated option for theft recovery.

## Replacing a Damaged Display

Displays get knocked off, rained on, and cracked by dropped bikes. Replacements cost $50–$300 depending on the brand and model. Before buying a bike, check:
- Is the display a standard mount (35 mm clamp) or proprietary?
- Are replacement displays available in Australia?
- Can you use the bike without the display (some bikes will not run the motor if the display is disconnected)?

## Bottom Line

A good display tells you battery level, speed, and assist level. Everything beyond that is a convenience, not a necessity. Apps add customisation and connectivity but should not drive your purchase decision. Choose the bike for its motor, battery, frame, and brakes — the display is the cherry on top.
