The bike itself is the foundation. Accessories make it practical, safe, and comfortable for daily use. Here are the accessories that actually matter for e-bike commuters — and the ones you can skip.

## Essential Accessories

### Phone Mount

Your phone is your navigation, fitness tracker, and music controller. A phone mount puts it in front of you — no fumbling in pockets, no pulling over to check directions.

**Types:**
- **Clamp mount (handlebar):** a universal clamp that grips the phone by its edges. Works with any phone and any case. Quick to install and remove. The most common type.
- **Magnetic mount:** a magnet on the stem or handlebar holds a magnetic case or plate on the back of the phone. Clean look, easy on/off. Less secure than a clamp on rough roads.
- **Quad Lock (twist-lock):** the phone case has a bayonet fitting that locks onto a dedicated mount. Very secure — the phone will not come off even on rough trails. Requires a specific case for your phone model.

**What to look for:**
- **Vibration damping:** e-bike motors and rough roads create vibration that can damage phone camera modules (the optical image stabiliser is sensitive to sustained vibration). Choose a mount with a rubber or silicone damper. Quad Lock sells a specific vibration dampener for this purpose.
- **Security:** the phone must stay on the mount over bumps, in rain, and during hard braking. A phone falling off at 25 km/h is expensive.
- **Waterproofing:** if you ride in rain, a waterproof phone pouch or a phone with an IP68 rating is essential.
- **Charging:** some mounts have a built-in USB charger powered by the e-bike's main battery. Useful for long rides where GPS navigation drains the phone battery.

### Mirror

A handlebar or helmet-mounted mirror lets you see traffic behind you without turning your head. This is more useful on an e-bike than a regular bike — at 25 km/h, taking your eyes off the road ahead for a shoulder check is riskier.

**Types:**
- **Bar-end mirror:** mounts inside the handlebar end. Clean look, minimal wind resistance, wide field of view. The best option for commuters.
- **Handlebar-mounted mirror:** clamps onto the handlebar. Easier to adjust than a bar-end mirror but sticks out and can catch on objects when parking.
- **Helmet-mounted mirror:** a small mirror on a stalk attached to the helmet. Moves with your head — look where you want to check. Takes practice to use and some riders find it distracting.

**Recommendation:** a bar-end mirror on the right side (traffic side in Australia). Check it regularly — it becomes instinctive after a few rides.

### Bell

Legally required on bicycles in most Australian states. A bell warns pedestrians and other cyclists of your approach — essential on shared paths where you are approaching at 20–25 km/h.

**Types:**
- **Ding bell:** the classic single-strike bell. Clear tone, easy to use, minimal hand movement.
- **Ring bell (continuous):** produces a sustained ringing when the lever is rotated. Louder and more attention-getting than a single ding.
- **Electronic horn:** battery-powered, very loud (100+ dB). Overkill for shared paths but useful on roads where car drivers need to hear you.

**Recommendation:** a quality ding bell (Knog Oi, Spurcycle, or Crane) — clear tone, compact, and does not make you look like you are trying to be a car.

### Mudguards (Fenders)

If you commute, you need mudguards. Without them, every wet road sprays a rooster tail of dirty water up your back, into your face, and over the entire drivetrain.

**Types:**
- **Full-length clip-on:** attach to the fork and seatpost without frame mounting points. Easy to install and remove. Adequate coverage but can rattle.
- **Full-length bolt-on:** mount to eyelets on the fork and frame. Best coverage, most secure, least rattle. The best option if your bike has the mounting points.
- **Stubby (short) fenders:** cover only a small section of the tyre. Better than nothing but still allow significant spray.

**Recommendation:** full-length bolt-on mudguards if your bike has eyelets. Full-length clip-on if it does not.

### Lock

Covered in detail in our parking and storage guide, but the minimum for commuting:
- **U-lock (D-lock)** through the frame and rear wheel, secured to a fixed object
- **Secondary cable** through the front wheel

Budget at least $80–$150 for a quality lock set. A $20 cable lock is theatre — it will be cut in 10 seconds.

## Recommended Accessories

### Pannier Bag or Rack Bag

Carrying gear on your body (backpack) makes you sweat. Carrying it on the bike (pannier) does not. A single pannier on the rear rack holds a laptop, lunch, change of clothes, and rain gear.

**What to look for:**
- **Waterproof** (roll-top closure or welded seams)
- **Quick-release mounting** (clip on and off the rack in seconds)
- **Reflective elements** (visibility from the side at night)
- **20–25 litre capacity** (enough for daily commuting without being oversized)

### Rear Rack

If your bike does not have one, a rear rack is worth adding. It enables panniers, rack bags, and bungee-strapped loads. Check:
- **Weight rating:** most racks are rated for 25 kg. Some heavy-duty racks handle 40 kg.
- **Compatibility:** the rack must fit your bike's frame (seat stays, seatpost, and axle type).
- **Material:** aluminium is lighter; steel is stronger and cheaper.

### Kickstand

Many e-bikes come with a kickstand; some do not. A 25 kg e-bike without a kickstand is a nuisance — you need a wall or railing every time you stop. A centre-mounted double-leg kickstand is the most stable option for heavy e-bikes.

## Nice-to-Have Accessories

### Handlebar Grips

The grips that come with most e-bikes are adequate but not great. Ergonomic grips with a flat palm rest reduce hand numbness and wrist strain on longer rides. Lock-on grips (with a bolt that secures them to the handlebar) do not slip or rotate.

### Bottle Cage

A water bottle cage mounted on the downtube keeps water within reach. Essential for summer commuting and any ride over 30 minutes. Some e-bikes have the battery where the bottle cage would go — in that case, a handlebar-mounted bottle holder works.

### Chain Guard or Chainstay Protector

A chain guard prevents your trouser leg from catching in the chain or getting greased. A chainstay protector (a rubber or neoprene strip on the chainstay) reduces chain-slap noise on rough roads.

### Saddle Cover

A waterproof saddle cover keeps the seat dry when the bike is parked outdoors. Sitting on a wet saddle is unpleasant.

## Accessories You Can Skip

- **Cycling computer:** your phone does everything a cycling computer does, and it is already on the handlebar mount
- **Spoke lights and wheel LEDs:** novelty, not safety. Good front and rear lights are sufficient.
- **Suspension seatpost (unless you have back issues):** most commuter routes are smooth enough that a standard seatpost is fine. Wider tyres at lower pressure provide more comfort than a suspension seatpost.
- **Aero bars or bar ends:** designed for road racing, not commuting. They change your hand position in a way that makes braking and steering slower — dangerous in traffic.

## Bottom Line

Start with the essentials: phone mount (with vibration damping), mirror, bell, mudguards, and a quality lock. Add a pannier and rear rack when you start commuting regularly. Everything else is optional — useful for some riders, unnecessary for others. Budget $200–$400 for accessories on top of the bike purchase.
