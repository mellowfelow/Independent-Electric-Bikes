# Commuting on an Electric Unicycle in Australia

Electric unicycles are the most capable and least understood personal electric vehicles on the market. A single wheel, a pair of foot platforms, and a gyroscopic balancing system combine to create a rideable that is remarkably portable, surprisingly fast, and genuinely practical for commuting. This guide covers what you need to know about using one as daily transport in Australia.

## What an Electric Unicycle Is

An electric unicycle (EUC) is a self-balancing device with a single large wheel, typically 14 to 22 inches in diameter. The rider stands on foot platforms on either side of the wheel and controls speed and braking by leaning forward and back. Turning is done by shifting weight and twisting the hips, similar to skiing.

There is no remote control and no handlebars. All input comes from the rider's body. This sounds impossible until you try it, and then it feels surprisingly natural, after a learning period.

## The Learning Curve

This is the elephant in the room. Learning to ride an EUC takes time and persistence.

**Week one.** Most people spend their first sessions holding a wall or railing and wobbling a few metres at a time. Expect to feel frustrated. Mounting and dismounting is the hardest part initially.

**Weeks two to three.** You can ride in a straight line on smooth ground without support. Turns are wide and hesitant. Speed is low.

**Weeks three to six.** Riding feels more natural. Turns tighten up. You start to develop the instinctive balance adjustments that make the EUC feel like an extension of your body.

**Beyond six weeks.** Confident riding on varied terrain, in traffic, and at commuting speeds. Advanced skills continue to develop for months.

Most riders who commit to daily practice of 30 to 60 minutes are commute-ready within four to six weeks. Riders with experience in balance sports (skating, skiing, surfing) often learn faster.

The learning curve is the biggest barrier to EUC commuting. If you are not willing to invest the time, a scooter or e-bike is a better choice.

## Why EUCs Excel for Commuting

Once you can ride, an EUC has several practical advantages over other personal electric vehicles.

**Portability.** An EUC rolls beside you on its own wheel when you are not riding, like a suitcase. Most have a retractable trolley handle for exactly this purpose. You can walk it through a shopping centre, roll it onto a train, and stow it under your desk. Try doing that with an e-bike.

**Speed and range.** Modern commuter EUCs cruise comfortably at 25 to 35 km/h with ranges of 30 to 80 km or more. This outperforms most electric scooters and skateboards.

**Terrain handling.** The large wheel rolls over bumps, cracks, and rough surfaces that would stop a scooter or skateboard. Australian suburban footpaths, which are often in poor condition, are manageable on an EUC with a 16-inch or larger wheel.

**Hands free.** With no handlebars or remote to hold, your hands are completely free while riding. You can carry a coffee, hold an umbrella in light rain, or simply let your arms swing naturally.

**Compact footprint.** An EUC takes up less space than any other electric vehicle. It fits in a car boot, under a desk, in a cupboard, or beside your feet on a train.

## Choosing an EUC for Commuting

**Wheel size.** For commuting on mixed Australian surfaces, a 16-inch wheel is the minimum. An 18-inch or 20-inch wheel handles rough roads and footpaths better and is more comfortable at speed. Smaller 14-inch wheels suit smooth, flat environments but struggle on typical suburban infrastructure.

**Battery capacity.** Choose a battery large enough to do your commute with at least 30 percent remaining. Running an EUC battery low reduces performance and increases the risk of a cutout (where the motor cannot maintain balance). For a 10 km commute each way, a battery of at least 800 Wh is sensible.

**Weight.** EUC weight ranges from about 12 kg for compact models to over 30 kg for high-performance units. For commuting, 15 to 22 kg is the practical range. Lighter is better for portability, but very light EUCs have smaller batteries and wheels.

**Suspension.** Some newer EUCs have built-in suspension, which significantly improves comfort on rough surfaces. If your commute includes poor-quality footpaths or road crossings, suspension is worth the extra cost and weight.

**Speed capability.** You want an EUC whose top speed is well above your cruising speed. This provides a safety margin: the motor always has reserve power to maintain balance. An EUC with a 50 km/h top speed ridden at 25 km/h has plenty of headroom. An EUC with a 30 km/h top speed ridden at 25 km/h is dangerously close to its limit.

## Australian Legal Status

EUCs exist in a legal grey area in most Australian states.

**Victoria** has enacted personal mobility device legislation with speed and power restrictions. Check VicRoads for current rules.

**Queensland** permits personal mobility devices on footpaths and shared paths under speed limits (typically 25 km/h).

**New South Wales** has historically been more restrictive regarding motorised personal transport on public paths and roads.

**Other states** vary. The regulatory landscape is evolving as these devices become more common.

The practical reality is that EUC riders commute daily in every major Australian city. Riding sensibly, yielding to pedestrians, and wearing safety gear reduces both risk and the likelihood of negative attention from authorities.

## Safety Gear

EUC commuting requires protective equipment. Falls happen, especially in the first months.

**Helmet.** Full-face helmets offer the best protection and are standard among experienced EUC riders. At commuting speeds of 25 to 35 km/h, a bicycle helmet is the minimum; a full-face is strongly recommended.

**Wrist guards.** Wrist injuries are common in EUC falls. Wrist guards are arguably the most important protective gear after a helmet.

**Knee and shin pads.** The wheel pedals can strike your shins during a dismount, and falls often land on knees. Integrated knee and shin guards are ideal.

**Elbow pads.** Protect against road rash and impact in sideways falls.

**Ankle support.** Some riders wear ankle braces or high-top shoes. The foot platforms can put strain on ankles during longer rides.

## Route Planning

**Surface quality matters more than distance.** A 5 km route on smooth bike paths is easier than a 3 km route on broken footpaths with frequent road crossings.

**Avoid busy roads.** EUCs are manoeuvrable but small and hard for drivers to see. Shared paths, bike lanes, and quiet streets are safer choices.

**Road crossings.** Practise kerb drops and kerb mounts. Many EUC riders learn to ride up and down kerbs smoothly, but road crossings with high kerbs may require dismounting.

**Wet surfaces.** EUCs handle light rain better than electric skateboards due to the larger wheel, but wet tiles, painted surfaces, and metal covers are still slippery. Reduce speed in wet conditions.

## Cost

EUCs for commuting range from about $1,200 for entry-level models to $4,000 or more for premium units with large batteries, suspension, and high-performance motors.

**Budget ($1,200 to $1,800).** Small to medium wheel (14 to 16 inch), adequate battery for short commutes, basic features. Fine for flat, smooth routes under 10 km each way.

**Mid-range ($1,800 to $3,000).** Larger wheel (16 to 18 inch), good battery capacity, better build quality. Handles most commuting scenarios.

**Premium ($3,000+).** Large wheel (18 to 22 inch), high-capacity battery, suspension, premium components. For longer commutes, rough terrain, or riders who want the best experience.

Add $200 to $400 for a full set of safety gear.

## Is an EUC Right for Your Commute

An EUC commute works well if you are willing to invest the learning time, your route is 3 to 15 km each way, you have mixed-quality surfaces that benefit from a large wheel, and portability for public transport integration matters.

It works less well if you need to be commuting immediately (the learning curve is real), your workplace does not allow you to store the device, or you are unwilling to wear protective gear.

For riders who get past the learning curve, an EUC often becomes their preferred form of urban transport. The combination of speed, range, portability, and terrain capability is unmatched by any other personal electric vehicle.
