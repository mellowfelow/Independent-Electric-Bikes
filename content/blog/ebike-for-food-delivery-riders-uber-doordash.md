Food delivery on an e-bike is one of the most demanding use cases for any electric bicycle. You ride 40–80 km per shift, in all weather, through traffic, carrying hot food that must arrive intact. The bike is a tool — it needs to be reliable, fast, and comfortable for hours of continuous use.

## Why E-Bikes Dominate Delivery

Delivery riders on e-bikes earn more per hour than those on regular bikes or on foot — and often more than those in cars (after fuel, insurance, registration, and depreciation).

**Advantages over regular bikes:**
- Cover more distance per hour (25 km/h vs 18 km/h average)
- Arrive less exhausted — ride longer shifts, accept more orders
- Handle hills without slowing down — critical in hilly cities like Sydney and Melbourne

**Advantages over cars:**
- No fuel cost ($0.15/charge vs $15–$25/shift for petrol)
- No parking delays (park at the restaurant door)
- No registration or insurance cost
- Faster in congested CBD traffic (bike lanes bypass gridlock)
- No traffic fines (no speeding, no illegal U-turns)

## What to Look For in a Delivery E-Bike

### Battery Capacity: 600 Wh Minimum

Delivery riders cover 40–80 km per shift, often with frequent stops and starts (which drain more battery than cruising). A 500 Wh battery is marginal — you will run out on long shifts or hilly routes.

**Recommended:** 600 Wh minimum, 700+ Wh preferred. A spare battery ($400–$800) is a smart investment — swap at the halfway point and never worry about range.

### Motor: Torque Over Speed

Stop-start urban delivery demands high torque at low speed (pulling away from lights with a loaded bike) more than high top speed. A motor with 50+ Nm of torque makes acceleration effortless.

**Mid-drive motors** are more efficient in stop-start riding because they leverage the bike's gears. But they accelerate chain and cassette wear — expect to replace chains every 1,500–2,500 km on a heavily used delivery bike.

**Hub motors** are simpler and cheaper to maintain. Less efficient in stop-start but adequate for flat cities.

### Brakes: Hydraulic Disc, 180 mm Rotors

You are braking dozens of times per hour in traffic. Mechanical brakes fatigue your hands and wear out quickly. Hydraulic disc brakes with 180 mm rotors provide strong, consistent stopping power all shift long.

### Tyres: Puncture-Resistant

A flat tyre during a delivery shift means cancelled orders, lost income, and a frustrating roadside repair. Install puncture-resistant tyres (Schwalbe Marathon Plus or Continental Contact Plus) and check pressure before every shift.

### Frame: Step-Through or Low Step-Over

You mount and dismount the bike dozens of times per shift. A step-through frame lets you hop on and off without swinging your leg over — faster, easier, and less likely to catch on the food bag.

### Lights: Always On

Delivery shifts often run into darkness. Integrated lights (powered by the main battery) mean you never forget to charge your lights. If your bike does not have integrated lights, invest in high-lumen USB-rechargeable lights and charge them between shifts.

## Carrying Food

### Insulated Delivery Bag

The platform provides the bag (Uber Eats, DoorDash, Menulog all issue insulated bags). The bag goes:

- **On a rear rack:** most stable, keeps the food level, and your back is free. Use bungee cords or a rack-mounted quick-release system to secure the bag.
- **On your back:** the default but not ideal. The bag shifts in corners, makes your back sweat, and raises your centre of gravity. Use the rear rack if your bike has one.

### Front Basket

A front basket carries drinks and small orders while the main bag sits on the rack. Useful for multi-order pickups.

### Keeping Food Level

Hot food and drinks spill when the bike tilts in corners or over bumps. Tips:
- Ride smoothly — avoid sharp turns and sudden braking
- Use a flat-bottomed bag that sits level on the rack
- Place drinks in a secondary bag with padding (bubble wrap or towels)
- Slow down over speed bumps and potholes

## Maintenance Schedule for Delivery Bikes

Delivery bikes cover 200–400 km per week — 10–20 times more than a casual rider. Maintenance must be more frequent:

| Component | Casual rider | Delivery rider |
|---|---|---|
| Chain check and lube | Monthly | Weekly |
| Chain replacement | Every 3,000–5,000 km | Every 1,500–2,500 km |
| Brake pad check | Monthly | Weekly |
| Tyre pressure check | Weekly | Before every shift |
| Tyre replacement | Annually | Every 3–6 months |
| Full service (bike shop) | Every 6 months | Every 2–3 months |
| Battery health check | Annually | Every 6 months |

Skipping maintenance leads to breakdowns during shifts — which cost you more in lost earnings than the maintenance itself.

## Earning Potential and Bike ROI

A delivery e-bike costing $2,000–$3,500 pays for itself quickly:

- Average delivery earnings: $18–$28/hour (varies by platform, city, and time)
- Average shift length: 4–6 hours
- Average shifts per week: 3–5
- Weekly earnings: $200–$700

At the lower end ($200/week), a $2,500 e-bike pays for itself in 12–13 weeks. At the higher end ($500/week), it pays for itself in 5 weeks.

Running costs (electricity, tyres, maintenance) are $20–$40 per week — compared to $100–$150 per week for a car doing the same work.

## Safety

Delivery riding is higher-risk than casual cycling:
- You ride in traffic for hours, not minutes
- You ride in rain, at night, and in peak-hour congestion
- Time pressure encourages risk-taking (running yellow lights, riding on footpaths, cutting corners)

**Safety rules for delivery riders:**
1. Wear a helmet — every ride, no exceptions
2. Use lights — front and rear, always on, even during the day
3. Do not check the phone while riding — pull over to accept or navigate orders
4. Obey traffic signals — no order is worth a collision
5. Wear high-visibility clothing — especially at night
6. Take breaks — fatigue increases accident risk. A 10-minute break every 2 hours keeps you alert

## Bottom Line

An e-bike is the best vehicle for food delivery in Australian cities — faster than walking, cheaper than driving, and more capable than a regular bike. Choose a bike with a large battery (600+ Wh), puncture-resistant tyres, hydraulic disc brakes, and a rear rack. Maintain it aggressively — the bike earns its keep only if it runs reliably every shift.
