The manufacturer says 80 km. You get 45. That is not a lie — it is physics.

## Why Rated Range Is Always Optimistic

Manufacturers test range under ideal conditions:
- Flat terrain
- No wind
- 70 kg rider
- Eco/low assist mode
- Moderate temperature (20 °C)
- Smooth, sealed road
- Constant speed (no stop-start)

Real-world riding includes hills, wind, heavier riders, higher assist levels, cold weather, rough surfaces, and frequent stops. Every one of these reduces range.

## The Factors That Determine Range

### 1. Battery Capacity (Wh)

This is the baseline. More watt-hours = more energy = more range.

| Battery (Wh) | Flat/Eco range | Hilly/Turbo range |
|---|---|---|
| 300 Wh | 40–60 km | 20–30 km |
| 400 Wh | 55–80 km | 25–40 km |
| 500 Wh | 70–100 km | 35–50 km |
| 625 Wh | 85–120 km | 45–65 km |
| 750 Wh | 100–140 km | 55–75 km |

These are approximate — every other factor on this list moves the number up or down.

### 2. Assist Level

The assist level is the single biggest controllable factor. The difference between Eco and Turbo is dramatic:

| Assist level | Motor power contribution | Typical range multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| Eco / Level 1 | 40–60 % of your effort | 1.0× (baseline) |
| Tour / Level 2 | 100–120 % of your effort | 0.7× |
| Sport / Level 3 | 170–210 % of your effort | 0.5× |
| Turbo / Level 4 | 250–340 % of your effort | 0.35× |

A 500 Wh battery that gives 90 km in Eco might give 30–35 km in Turbo.

**Practical tip:** use Eco on flat terrain and tailwinds, Sport or Turbo on hills and headwinds. Mixing modes optimises range without sacrificing comfort when you need the power.

### 3. Terrain

Hills are the biggest range killer after assist level.

- **Climbing consumes 2–4× more energy per kilometre than flat riding.** The motor works harder, for longer, against gravity.
- **Descending recovers little energy.** Unless your bike has regenerative braking (and even then, regen recovers only 5–10 %), the energy spent climbing is largely lost.
- **A hilly 20 km route may use as much battery as a flat 50 km route.**

### 4. Rider Weight

Heavier riders (and heavier bikes) require more energy to accelerate and climb. A 100 kg rider will get 15–25 % less range than a 70 kg rider on the same bike, same route, same assist level.

**Cargo weight counts too.** A 15 kg load of groceries or tools reduces range by 5–10 %.

### 5. Wind

A headwind at 20 km/h roughly doubles the air resistance compared to calm conditions. The motor works harder to maintain speed, draining the battery faster.

**Crosswinds** are less impactful on range but affect handling — especially on a heavy e-bike with panniers acting as sails.

**Tailwinds** are free energy. On a windy day, plan your route to ride with the wind on the return leg (when you are more likely to be low on battery).

### 6. Temperature

Cold batteries deliver less energy. At 0 °C, a lithium-ion battery may deliver 10–20 % less range than at 20 °C. At -10 °C, the loss can be 20–30 %.

Heat also reduces range slightly (the motor and controller are less efficient when hot), but the effect is smaller than cold.

**Melbourne winter:** expect 10–15 % less range on cold mornings (5–10 °C). The battery warms up during the ride, so the first few kilometres are the most affected.

### 7. Tyre Pressure and Width

Under-inflated tyres increase rolling resistance — the motor works harder to maintain speed.

- Check tyre pressure weekly
- Inflate to the pressure recommended on the tyre sidewall (typically 40–65 PSI for commuter tyres, 20–30 PSI for fat tyres)
- Wider tyres have more rolling resistance than narrow tyres (but more comfort and grip)

### 8. Speed

Air resistance increases with the square of speed. Riding at 28 km/h (pedalling beyond the motor cut-off) uses roughly 25 % more energy than cruising at 22 km/h.

**The sweet spot for range:** 18–22 km/h in Eco mode. You are moving efficiently without fighting significant air resistance.

### 9. Stop-Start Riding

Accelerating from zero to 25 km/h uses far more energy than cruising at 25 km/h. City riding with traffic lights every 200 metres drains the battery faster than a continuous ride on a bike path.

**Tip:** anticipate stops. Coast to a stop rather than braking hard and then accelerating hard. Smooth riding extends range.

### 10. Motor Type

- **Mid-drive motors** are more efficient on hilly terrain (they use gears) but less efficient at constant speed on flat ground.
- **Hub motors** are less efficient on hills but adequate on flat terrain.
- **Direct-drive hub motors** are the least efficient (they have cogging drag when the motor is off) but offer regenerative braking.

## How to Maximise Range

1. **Start in Eco** and only increase assist when you need it (hills, headwinds, fatigue)
2. **Maintain steady speed** — avoid hard acceleration and heavy braking
3. **Keep tyres inflated** to the recommended pressure
4. **Pedal consistently** — the motor is most efficient when you are contributing effort
5. **Choose a sheltered route** on windy days
6. **Keep the battery warm** — start with the battery at room temperature, not cold
7. **Shift gears on a mid-drive** — use lower gears on hills (the motor is more efficient at higher RPM)
8. **Reduce weight** — carry only what you need. Every kilogram matters on hills.

## Range Anxiety and Planning

### Know Your Commute

Measure your actual commute range by riding it on a full battery and noting how much charge you use. Do this in both directions (one may be hillier or windier). Add 20 % buffer — you do not want to run out on a windy day or a detour.

### Charge at Work

If your commute is long (30+ km each way), charge the battery at work. Most e-bike chargers are small enough to carry in a pannier, and a standard wall outlet is all you need.

### Spare Battery

For very long commutes, delivery riders, or touring, a spare battery ($400–$1,000) doubles your range. Swap batteries at the halfway point.

## Bottom Line

Rated range is a best-case number. Expect 50–70 % of the rated range in typical Australian conditions (moderate hills, mixed assist levels, 80 kg rider). A 500 Wh battery gives a realistic 35–70 km depending on terrain and assist. For a daily commute, you need a battery that covers your round trip with a 20 % buffer. If your commute is 15 km each way (30 km round trip), a 400 Wh battery is adequate; 500 Wh gives comfortable margin.
