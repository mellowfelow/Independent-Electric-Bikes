Riding an e-bike at night introduces risks that do not exist during the day: reduced visibility, impaired depth perception, wildlife, and drivers who are not looking for cyclists. The right lights, reflectors, and habits turn night riding from dangerous to routine.

## Legal Requirements

In every Australian state, riding at night requires:
- **A steady or flashing white front light** visible from 200 metres
- **A steady or flashing red rear light** visible from 200 metres
- **A red rear reflector** visible from 50 metres

These are the legal minimums. They are not enough for safe night riding.

## Front Lights: How Many Lumens?

Lumens measure total light output. More lumens = brighter = more visible and better road illumination.

| Lumens | Use case |
|---|---|
| 100–200 | Be-seen only (daytime visibility, well-lit urban streets at night) |
| 400–600 | Urban commuting on lit streets |
| 800–1200 | Suburban commuting on partially lit roads |
| 1200–2000 | Rural riding, unlit bike paths, trail riding |
| 2000+ | Off-road night riding, mountain biking |

For most Australian commuters riding on lit urban streets, **400–800 lumens** is the practical range. If any part of your route is unlit (bike paths through parks, suburban streets without streetlights), step up to **800–1200 lumens**.

### Beam Pattern

Lumens tell you how much light, but beam pattern tells you where it goes.

- **Flood beam:** wide, even spread. Good for seeing the road surface and being seen by traffic from the side. Less focused for seeing far ahead.
- **Spot beam:** concentrated forward beam. Good for seeing far down the road but poor peripheral illumination.
- **Shaped beam (StVZO-compliant):** a German automotive-style beam pattern with a sharp cut-off at the top to avoid blinding oncoming traffic. The best option for shared-path riding where you face pedestrians and other cyclists. Brands like Busch + Müller and Lupine specialise in this pattern.

**Recommendation:** for commuting, choose a light with a **flood or shaped beam** in the 400–800 lumen range. A spot beam is better for trail riding where blinding oncoming users is not a concern.

### Mounting

- **Handlebar mount:** the standard position. Illuminates the road ahead. Easy to aim, easy to remove for charging.
- **Helmet mount:** the light follows your gaze — look into a corner and the light follows. Best as a secondary light alongside a handlebar-mounted primary.
- **Fork mount:** mounted low on the fork or front rack. Creates long shadows that reveal surface texture (bumps, cracks, potholes). Excellent for seeing road hazards.

**Best setup for commuting:** one handlebar-mounted flood light (primary) plus a fork-mounted or second handlebar light (secondary, for surface texture).

## Rear Lights

Your rear light keeps you visible to traffic approaching from behind — the most dangerous direction for a cyclist.

### Brightness

- **Minimum 50 lumens** for night riding
- **100+ lumens** for riding on roads with fast traffic
- **Flash mode** is more visible to drivers than steady mode (the movement catches attention), but use a steady light as well — flash-only makes it harder for drivers to judge your distance and speed

### Features

- **Brake light function:** some lights detect deceleration (via accelerometer) and brighten automatically. This signals to following traffic that you are slowing down.
- **USB rechargeable:** mandatory for commuting. Battery-powered lights die at the worst times.
- **Integrated into the seatpost or rack:** harder to steal than a clip-on light. Some e-bikes have built-in rear lights powered by the main battery.
- **Side visibility:** lights with amber side LEDs or a wide-angle lens are visible at intersections, where most car-bike collisions occur.

## Reflectors and Reflective Gear

Reflectors are passive — they only work when hit by a car's headlights. But they work without batteries, without charging, and without remembering to turn them on.

### Wheel Reflectors

Spoke-mounted reflectors or reflective tyre sidewalls make you visible from the side. This matters at intersections, where a driver turning across your path needs to see you from a 90-degree angle — where your front and rear lights are invisible.

### Clothing

- **Reflective jacket or vest:** high-visibility yellow or orange with reflective strips. Visible from 200+ metres in headlights.
- **Reflective ankle bands:** the movement of your pedalling legs catches a driver's eye more effectively than a static reflective surface.
- **Reflective helmet cover or stickers:** makes your head visible, which is the highest point on the bike and the most likely to be in a driver's field of view above parked cars.

### Bike Reflectors

- Front white reflector
- Rear red reflector
- Pedal reflectors (amber, one on each pedal — legally required in most states)
- Spoke reflectors or reflective tape on the rims

## Night Riding Techniques

### Speed

Ride slower at night. Your ability to see and react to hazards is reduced by 30–50 % compared to daylight. What you cannot see, you cannot avoid — potholes, debris, wildlife, pedestrians in dark clothing.

### Predictability

Be even more predictable than during the day. Signal every turn, hold a straight line, and avoid sudden moves. Drivers have less visual information about you at night — help them anticipate your movements.

### Eye Adjustment

Your eyes need 10–20 minutes to fully adjust to darkness. If you ride from a brightly lit office into a dark street, your night vision is impaired for the first few minutes. Start slowly and let your eyes adjust.

Do not look directly at oncoming headlights — they destroy your night vision temporarily. Look at the road surface or the left edge of the road to maintain peripheral vision.

### Wildlife

Kangaroos, wombats, possums, and foxes are most active at dawn and dusk. On suburban and rural routes:
- Scan the roadside for eye reflections (animals' eyes reflect light)
- Slow down near bushland, parks, and creek crossings
- Be prepared to stop — a kangaroo can jump onto the road with zero warning

## Bottom Line

Night riding on an e-bike is safe with the right setup: a 400+ lumen front light, a 50+ lumen rear light with flash mode, reflective clothing, and reduced speed. The total cost of a quality light set and reflective gear is $80–$200 — a tiny fraction of the bike's value and a massive improvement in safety. If you commute year-round in Melbourne or any southern Australian city, you will ride in the dark for 4–5 months of the year. Invest in lights on day one.
