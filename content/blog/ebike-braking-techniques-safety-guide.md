Braking on an e-bike is different from braking on a regular bike. You are heavier (bike + battery + motor = 20–26 kg), faster (motor-assisted to 25 km/h), and the weight distribution changes with the motor and battery placement. Here is how to stop safely every time.

## The Physics of E-Bike Braking

### More Weight = Longer Stopping Distance
A regular bike + rider weighs approximately 80–90 kg. An e-bike + rider weighs 95–115 kg. That extra 15–25 kg means 15–25 % more momentum at any given speed — and momentum is what brakes must overcome.

**Stopping distance at 25 km/h on dry road:**
- Regular bike: approximately 5–7 metres
- E-bike: approximately 7–10 metres

### Weight Transfer
When you brake, weight shifts forward. On an e-bike, the heavier starting weight means more weight transfers to the front wheel — increasing the risk of the rear wheel lifting or the front wheel locking.

## Front Brake vs Rear Brake

### Front Brake
Provides 60–70 % of your stopping power. During braking, weight transfers forward — the front tyre presses harder into the road, giving it more grip. The front brake can stop you faster than the rear brake alone.

**Risk:** too much front brake locks the front wheel, causing a loss of steering and a potential forward pitch (going over the handlebars). On an e-bike, the heavier weight reduces this risk slightly (more weight keeps the rear wheel grounded), but it is still possible on steep descents.

### Rear Brake
Provides 30–40 % of your stopping power. During braking, weight transfers off the rear wheel — reducing its grip. The rear brake is less powerful but more forgiving: a locked rear wheel skids but does not cause you to pitch forward.

**Risk:** too much rear brake causes a skid. A skid reduces braking effectiveness and can cause a loss of control, especially on wet or loose surfaces.

### How to Use Both

**Default technique: 70/30 front/rear.**
Apply both brakes simultaneously, with more force on the front. This maximises stopping power while keeping the rear wheel grounded.

**Emergency stop:** apply both brakes firmly. Shift your weight backward (push your hips behind the saddle) to keep the rear wheel grounded. Do not grab the front brake suddenly — squeeze it progressively.

**Wet conditions:** reduce front brake pressure (50/50 front/rear). Wet roads reduce tyre grip — a front wheel lock is more likely and more dangerous in the wet.

**Loose surfaces (gravel, dirt):** reduce front brake pressure (40/60 front/rear). On loose surfaces, the front wheel can wash out if locked. More rear brake is safer.

**Steep descents:** more rear brake (50/50 or 40/60 front/rear). On a steep descent, weight is already shifted forward — too much front brake lifts the rear wheel. Use both brakes moderately and control speed before the descent, not during it.

## Braking Techniques

### Progressive Braking
Squeeze the brake levers progressively — do not grab them suddenly. Start with light pressure and increase. This allows the tyres to build grip gradually and prevents lockups.

**Think:** squeeze, not grab.

### Braking Before Corners
Brake before the corner, not in it. Reduce your speed to a comfortable cornering speed, then release the brakes and lean through the turn. Braking while leaned over reduces the tyre's cornering grip and can cause a slide.

### Scrubbing Speed on Descents
On long descents, alternate between braking and releasing (pump the brakes). Do not drag the brakes continuously — this overheats the brake pads and rotors, reducing braking power (brake fade). Disc brakes are more resistant to fade than rim brakes, but they can still overheat on extended descents.

### One-Finger Braking
Modern hydraulic disc brakes are powerful enough to stop an e-bike with one finger on each lever. Using one finger (index finger) keeps the other three fingers on the grip for control. Two-finger braking is also fine — but avoid grabbing the lever with your whole hand (less control, more likely to lock the wheel).

## Disc Brakes: Mechanical vs Hydraulic

### Mechanical Disc
A cable pulls the brake pad against the rotor. Adequate stopping power, but:
- Requires more hand force than hydraulic
- Cable stretches over time — needs regular adjustment
- Single-piston design (most mechanical discs) applies force from one side only — less even pad wear

### Hydraulic Disc
Fluid pressure pushes the brake pads against the rotor. Superior stopping power:
- Less hand force required (important for riders with smaller hands or reduced grip strength)
- Self-adjusting (pads move closer to the rotor as they wear)
- Dual-piston design applies force from both sides — more even braking and pad wear
- Better modulation (easier to apply progressive, precise braking force)

**Recommendation for e-bikes:** hydraulic disc brakes. The heavier weight and higher speeds of e-bikes demand the stronger, more consistent stopping power of hydraulics. If your e-bike came with mechanical discs, upgrading to hydraulic ($100–$200) is the best safety upgrade you can make.

## Rotor Size

Larger rotors provide more stopping power (more leverage on the wheel).

| Rotor size | Use |
|---|---|
| 160 mm | Light riding, flat terrain, lighter riders |
| 180 mm | Standard for e-bike commuting — adequate for most riders |
| 203 mm | Heavy riders, mountain biking, loaded touring, steep descents |

**E-bike recommendation:** 180 mm minimum on both wheels. 203 mm front / 180 mm rear for mountain e-bikes, cargo bikes, and heavy riders.

## Brake Maintenance

### Pad Check (Monthly)
Look through the caliper at the brake pads. If the pad material is thinner than 1 mm, replace the pads. E-bike brake pads wear faster than regular bike pads — budget for replacement every 3,000–5,000 km.

### Rotor Check (Monthly)
Check the rotor for warping: spin the wheel and watch the rotor pass through the caliper. If it wobbles, the rotor is warped. A slight warp can be trued with a rotor truing tool; a severe warp requires replacement.

### Contamination
Oil, grease, lubricant, or cleaning products on the brake rotor or pads destroy braking performance. If your brakes suddenly feel weak or make a glassy squealing sound:
- Clean the rotor with isopropyl alcohol
- If the pads are contaminated, replace them — cleaning pads rarely works
- Never spray lubricant near the brakes. When cleaning the chain, cover the rotor with a rag.

### Hydraulic Brake Bleed (Annually)
Hydraulic brake fluid absorbs moisture over time, reducing braking performance. A brake bleed replaces the fluid. Have this done at a bike shop during your annual service ($30–$60 per brake).

## Motor Cut-Off

E-bikes cut motor power when you apply the brakes. This is a safety feature — you do not want the motor pushing you forward while you are trying to stop.

**How it works:** sensors in the brake levers (magnetic switches) detect when you pull the lever and send a signal to the controller to cut power. If the motor does not cut when you brake, check the brake sensor alignment (the magnet near the lever may have shifted).

## Bottom Line

E-bike braking requires more attention than regular bike braking because of the extra weight and speed. Use both brakes (70/30 front/rear on dry road), squeeze progressively (never grab), brake before corners, and pump the brakes on long descents. Hydraulic disc brakes are strongly recommended for e-bikes. Check pads monthly, keep rotors clean, and bleed the brakes annually. The habit of braking correctly is the single most important safety skill on an e-bike.
