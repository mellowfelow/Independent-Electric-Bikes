The most common objection to e-bikes: "That's cheating — you're not exercising." The research says otherwise.

## What the Science Says

Multiple peer-reviewed studies have measured the physical effort of e-bike riding compared to regular cycling and other activities:

**Study: University of Basel (2018)**
Sedentary, overweight adults rode e-bikes for 4 weeks. Results: improved cardiovascular fitness, lower body fat, and improved blood sugar levels — comparable to the improvements seen in the group riding regular bikes.

**Study: Brigham Young University (2019)**
Heart rate data from experienced mountain bikers showed e-MTB riding provided 94 % of the cardiovascular workout of regular mountain biking. The e-bike riders covered more distance and climbed more elevation — they just did it at a slightly lower heart rate.

**Study: Transport Research Laboratory (UK, 2019)**
E-bike commuters in a year-long study showed higher overall physical activity levels than non-cyclists — and similar activity levels to regular cyclists. The reason: e-bike riders rode more often and for longer distances because the bike made cycling accessible and enjoyable.

**The key finding across studies:** e-bikes provide moderate-intensity exercise that is sufficient for meaningful health benefits. The motor does not eliminate exercise — it reduces the peaks (steep hills, headwinds, fatigue) while maintaining a baseline of physical effort.

## How Much Exercise Are You Actually Getting?

### Heart Rate

On a regular bike at moderate effort: 130–160 BPM (65–80 % of max heart rate)
On an e-bike at the same speed with medium assist: 110–140 BPM (55–70 % of max heart rate)

The e-bike rider works at a lower intensity — but still well within the zone that the WHO (World Health Organisation) classifies as "moderate-intensity aerobic activity."

### Calories

A 75 kg rider commuting 10 km:
- Regular bike: 250–350 calories
- E-bike (medium assist): 150–250 calories
- Driving: 0 calories (plus the walk from the car park, maybe 20 calories)
- Public transport: 0 calories (plus the walk to/from the stop, maybe 30–50 calories)

The e-bike burns fewer calories per ride than a regular bike — but far more than any motorised alternative. And because e-bike riders ride more often, the total weekly calorie burn is often equal to or higher than regular cyclists.

### WHO Guidelines

The WHO recommends 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week for adults.

A 30-minute e-bike commute (each way) on 5 days per week = **300 minutes of moderate exercise** — double the minimum recommendation.

## Health Benefits

### Cardiovascular
Regular e-bike riding improves:
- Resting heart rate (lower is better)
- VO2 max (the body's ability to use oxygen — a key fitness marker)
- Blood pressure (lower)
- Blood lipid profile (lower LDL cholesterol, higher HDL)

### Metabolic
- Improved insulin sensitivity (reduced diabetes risk)
- Lower body fat percentage
- Better blood sugar regulation
- Weight management (e-bike commuting burns 700–1,200 calories per week at medium assist)

### Mental Health
- Reduced symptoms of anxiety and depression (outdoor exercise is consistently effective)
- Improved mood and energy levels (the post-ride endorphin effect)
- Better sleep quality
- Reduced stress (riding replaces the stress of driving in traffic or waiting for delayed trains)

### Joint Health
E-bikes are particularly good for joint health because:
- Cycling is low-impact (no pounding on knees, hips, or ankles like running)
- The motor reduces the force required on each pedal stroke — less stress on knees
- Riders with arthritis, knee replacements, or joint problems can ride an e-bike when a regular bike would be too painful
- The bike supports your weight (unlike walking or running)

## Who Benefits Most?

### Older Adults
E-bikes enable older adults to continue cycling (or start cycling) when age-related fitness decline makes regular cycling impractical. The motor compensates for reduced strength and endurance. Studies show e-bikes improve cognitive function, balance, and social engagement in older adults.

### Overweight and Obese Riders
Starting exercise is the hardest part. An e-bike reduces the barrier: you do not need to be fit to start, you do not arrive exhausted, and you can gradually reduce the assist level as fitness improves. The weight-bearing capacity of most e-bikes (100–120 kg rider weight) accommodates larger riders.

### People Recovering from Injury or Illness
E-bikes provide graduated exercise — start with high assist (minimal effort) and reduce assist as strength returns. Physiotherapists increasingly recommend e-bikes for cardiac rehabilitation, post-surgery recovery, and chronic disease management.

### People Who Do Not Exercise
The most important benefit of e-bikes is not that they provide exercise — it is that they convert non-exercisers into exercisers. The motor makes cycling enjoyable enough that people who would never ride a regular bike start riding daily. The best exercise is the one you actually do.

## The "Cheating" Argument

The idea that e-bikes are "cheating" assumes that the goal of cycling is maximum physical effort. For competitive cyclists, that is true. For everyone else, the goal is usually transport, health, or enjoyment — and an e-bike delivers all three.

**Consider:**
- Nobody calls a car "cheating" compared to walking
- Nobody calls an escalator "cheating" compared to stairs
- Nobody calls a washing machine "cheating" compared to hand-washing

An e-bike is a tool that makes cycling accessible to people who would otherwise drive. The comparison is not e-bike vs regular bike — it is e-bike vs car. And by that measure, the e-bike is a massive health upgrade.

## Improving Fitness on an E-Bike

If you want to use your e-bike as a fitness tool:

1. **Start with high assist.** Build the habit of riding every day. Fitness follows consistency.
2. **Gradually reduce assist level.** Over weeks and months, shift from Turbo to Sport to Tour to Eco. Your body adapts, and what felt hard at Eco eventually feels normal.
3. **Extend your route.** Add 1–2 km every few weeks. The motor makes longer rides accessible before you are "ready" for them on a regular bike.
4. **Track your progress.** Use a fitness app (Strava, Komoot, or the e-bike's own app) to track distance, speed, elevation, and heart rate. Watching the numbers improve is motivating.
5. **Add intervals.** Ride at low assist for 2 minutes, then high assist for 1 minute (recovery). This mimics interval training and improves cardiovascular fitness faster than steady-state riding.

## Bottom Line

E-bikes provide meaningful, scientifically validated exercise — enough to meet WHO guidelines for physical activity, reduce cardiovascular disease risk, improve mental health, and manage weight. They are not as intense as regular cycling, but they are far more effective than the alternative (driving or taking public transport). The motor does not eliminate exercise — it makes exercise accessible, enjoyable, and consistent. That consistency is what produces health benefits.
