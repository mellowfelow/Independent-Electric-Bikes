One of the best things about e-bikes is how quiet they are. No engine roar, no exhaust rumble, no vibration through the frame. But "quiet" is not "silent" — every e-bike makes some noise, and the type and volume depends on the motor, drivetrain, and components. Here is what you should hear, what you should not hear, and how to fix the noises that should not be there.

## Normal E-Bike Noise Levels

### How Quiet Is an E-Bike?

A typical e-bike at cruising speed (20–25 km/h) produces 50–65 dB at the rider's ear. For comparison:

| Source | dB level |
|---|---|
| Library | 30–40 dB |
| Normal conversation | 60 dB |
| **E-bike (hub motor, cruising)** | **50–55 dB** |
| **E-bike (mid-drive, cruising)** | **55–65 dB** |
| Vacuum cleaner | 70 dB |
| Motorcycle (at speed) | 80–95 dB |
| Car (at 50 km/h) | 65–75 dB |

At 50–55 dB, a hub-motor e-bike is quieter than a normal conversation. You can ride past pedestrians without startling them (though a bell is still courteous and often legally required).

### Hub Motor Noise
Hub motors — especially direct-drive hub motors — are the quietest e-bike motors. The motor is sealed inside the wheel hub with no external moving parts. You hear:
- A faint electrical hum at high power (barely audible above wind and tyre noise)
- Geared hub motors add a quiet whirring from the internal planetary gears

### Mid-Drive Motor Noise
Mid-drive motors are slightly louder than hub motors because:
- The motor drives the chain, which adds mechanical noise
- Higher torque output vibrates the frame more
- The motor's internal gears produce a characteristic whine under load

Premium mid-drives (Bosch, Shimano STEPS, Fazua) are engineered for quiet operation — 55–60 dB under normal riding. Budget mid-drives can be louder (60–70 dB), with a more noticeable whine.

### Tyre Noise
Above 20 km/h, tyre noise is often louder than the motor. Wider, knobby tyres on rough surfaces produce 60–70 dB. Smooth, narrow tyres on sealed roads produce 45–55 dB.

### Wind Noise
Above 25 km/h, wind noise dominates (60–70 dB). At e-bike speeds (up to 25 km/h with assist), wind noise is present but not overwhelming.

## Abnormal Noises (and What They Mean)

If your e-bike starts making a new noise, something has changed — and it is usually worth investigating.

### Clicking from the Bottom Bracket Area
**Possible causes:**
- Loose crank bolts (tighten to the specified torque)
- Worn bottom bracket bearings (replace the bottom bracket)
- Loose pedal threads (remove, grease threads, reinstall, tighten)
- Loose chainring bolts (tighten)

**Test:** pedal with one foot at a time. If the click matches one side only, the cause is on that side (pedal, crank, or that side of the bottom bracket).

### Grinding from the Motor
**Possible causes:**
- Worn internal gears (geared hub motor — the nylon reduction gears wear out after 10,000–20,000 km)
- Damaged bearing (hub motor or mid-drive — bearings can fail from water ingress or overloading)
- Foreign object in the motor housing (rare, but possible on mid-drives if debris enters through the chainring area)

**Action:** have the motor inspected by a bike shop. Continuing to ride with grinding bearings causes further damage.

### High-Pitched Whine Under Load
**Possible causes:**
- Normal on some mid-drives (especially under high torque). Bosch Performance Line CX and Shimano EP8 both produce a characteristic whine at high assist levels — this is normal.
- Abnormal if the volume increases over time, or if it appeared suddenly (possible controller issue or loose motor mount).

### Chain Rattle
**Possible causes:**
- Chain too loose (adjust rear derailleur tension or replace a worn chain)
- Chain skipping on a worn cassette (replace chain + cassette together)
- Front derailleur rubbing (adjust the derailleur limit screws or cable tension)
- Chain slap on the chainstay (install a chainstay protector — a rubber or neoprene wrap that dampens chain vibration)

### Brake Squeal
**Possible causes:**
- Contaminated brake pads or rotor (oil, grease, or cleaning product on the braking surface). Clean the rotor with isopropyl alcohol; if the pads are contaminated, replace them.
- Glazed brake pads (from dragging the brakes on a long descent). Sand the pad surface lightly with fine sandpaper.
- Misaligned caliper (the pads are rubbing the rotor unevenly). Loosen the caliper bolts, squeeze the brake lever, retighten the bolts while the lever is held — this centres the caliper over the rotor.

### Creaking from the Seatpost or Handlebars
**Possible causes:**
- Dry seatpost in the frame (remove, clean, apply a thin layer of carbon paste or grease, reinstall)
- Dry handlebar in the stem clamp (same — clean and grease the contact surfaces)
- Loose stem bolts or seatpost clamp (tighten to the specified torque)

## Making Your E-Bike Quieter

### Drivetrain
- **Clean and lube the chain regularly.** A dry, dirty chain is louder than a clean, lubricated one. Use a quality chain lube — wet lube for commuting in rain, dry lube for dry conditions.
- **Replace worn chains and cassettes.** A stretched chain riding on worn cassette teeth generates clicking and skipping noise.
- **Consider a belt drive.** Gates Carbon Belt Drive is virtually silent, maintenance-free, and lasts 15,000–30,000 km. Available on internally-geared hub e-bikes (Enviolo, Shimano Nexus/Alfine). Not compatible with derailleur gearing.

### Motor
- **Tighten the motor mount bolts.** Loose motor mounts vibrate against the frame — check torque every 6 months.
- **Update the motor firmware.** Some manufacturers release firmware updates that improve motor smoothness and reduce noise. Check with your dealer or the manufacturer's app.

### Tyres
- **Choose smooth tyres for road riding.** Knobby tyres generate significantly more noise on sealed roads. If you ride mostly on pavement, switch to slick or semi-slick tyres.
- **Correct tyre pressure.** Over-inflated tyres are louder (more vibration transmitted through the rigid tyre). Run the manufacturer's recommended pressure, not maximum.

### Frame
- **Secure all cables and cable housings.** Loose cables rattle against the frame. Use cable clips or zip ties to secure them.
- **Install a chainstay protector.** Prevents chain slap noise on rough roads.
- **Check all bolts periodically.** Loose bolts are the most common cause of creaks and rattles. Use a torque wrench — do not over-tighten.

## Noise and Courtesy

E-bikes are quiet enough that pedestrians often do not hear you approaching from behind. This creates a safety issue on shared paths.

**Solutions:**
- **Use a bell.** Ring it 10–20 metres before passing. It is courteous and required by law in most Australian states.
- **Slow down near pedestrians.** Pass at walking speed, especially near children, dogs, and elderly people.
- **Say "passing on your left" (or right).** A friendly verbal warning is effective when a bell is not enough.

Do not rely on motor noise to announce your presence — on a hub-motor e-bike, pedestrians will not hear you until you are alongside them.

## Bottom Line

E-bikes are remarkably quiet — 50–65 dB, quieter than a car and often quieter than a conversation. Hub motors are the quietest; mid-drives are slightly louder but still far below any combustion vehicle. Abnormal noises (clicking, grinding, squealing) usually indicate a maintenance issue — investigate them early before they become expensive. Keep the drivetrain clean and lubricated, tighten bolts periodically, and use a bell on shared paths because pedestrians genuinely cannot hear you coming.
