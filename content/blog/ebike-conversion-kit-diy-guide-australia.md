A conversion kit turns your regular bicycle into an e-bike. It is cheaper than buying a complete e-bike, lets you keep a bike you already love, and is a satisfying DIY project. Here is what you need to know before you start.

## What Is a Conversion Kit?

A conversion kit adds a motor and battery to an existing bicycle. The basic kit includes:
- **Motor** (hub motor in a replacement wheel, or a mid-drive unit that mounts at the bottom bracket)
- **Battery** (lithium-ion, mounts on the frame or rear rack)
- **Controller** (the brain — manages power delivery from the battery to the motor)
- **Display** (shows speed, battery level, assist mode)
- **Throttle and/or pedal assist sensor** (tells the controller when to deliver power)
- **Wiring harness** (connects everything)

## Hub Motor vs Mid-Drive Kit

### Hub Motor Kit

The motor is built into a replacement wheel (front or rear). You swap your existing wheel for the motorised one.

**Front hub:**
- Easiest installation (swap the front wheel, run cables)
- Creates a two-wheel-drive effect (motor drives front, you pedal rear)
- Lighter load on the chain and drivetrain
- Can feel unstable on loose surfaces (front wheel spinning under power)

**Rear hub:**
- More natural traction (power on the driven wheel)
- Better hill climbing than front hub
- Harder to install (need to route the chain, cassette, and derailleur around the motor)
- Adds weight to the rear (can feel rear-heavy)

**Hub motor advantages:** simpler installation, lower cost ($300–$800), no drivetrain wear from the motor, compatible with most bikes.

**Hub motor disadvantages:** heavier (2–4 kg for the motor wheel), does not benefit from gears (motor spins the wheel directly), less natural pedal feel.

### Mid-Drive Kit

The motor mounts at the bottom bracket and drives the chain — just like your legs do.

**Mid-drive advantages:** drives through the gears (efficient on hills), lighter wheels, more natural pedal feel, better weight distribution (motor is low and central).

**Mid-drive disadvantages:** harder to install (bottom bracket compatibility is critical), more expensive ($500–$1,500), accelerates chain and cassette wear, not compatible with all frame geometries.

**Common mid-drive kits:** Bafang BBS01 (250 W), Bafang BBS02 (750 W — not legal on Australian roads without restricting to 250 W), Bafang BBSHD (1,000 W — off-road only), Tongsheng TSDZ2 (torque-sensing, natural feel).

## Legal Requirements in Australia

To ride legally on Australian roads, your converted e-bike must comply with:

- **Motor power:** maximum 250 W continuous rated output
- **Assist type:** pedal assist (the motor only works when you pedal). A throttle is permitted on some models but the bike must still meet the power and speed limits.
- **Speed cut-off:** motor assist must cut off at 25 km/h
- **No registration or licence required** if the above conditions are met

**Important:** many kits sold online are rated at 500 W, 750 W, or 1,000 W. These are not legal for road use in Australia. You can buy a higher-power kit and restrict it to 250 W / 25 km/h via the controller settings, but this is your responsibility — if caught riding an unrestricted over-powered e-bike, you face fines and the bike may be impounded.

## Choosing a Kit

### Compatibility Checks

Before buying a kit, check:

1. **Wheel size:** hub motor kits come in specific wheel sizes (20", 26", 27.5", 700c, 29"). Match the kit to your bike's wheel size.
2. **Dropout width:** the distance between the fork tips (front) or frame dropouts (rear). Standard is 100 mm front, 135 mm rear. Measure yours — some bikes differ.
3. **Axle type:** quick release or thru-axle? Most kits are designed for quick release. Thru-axle adapters exist but add complexity.
4. **Bottom bracket (mid-drive only):** measure the BB shell width (68 mm, 73 mm, or 83 mm) and type (threaded BSA, press-fit). Most Bafang kits fit 68–73 mm BSA shells.
5. **Frame clearance:** will the battery fit on the frame? Down tube batteries need bottle cage mounts and adequate triangle space. Rear rack batteries need a compatible rack.
6. **Brake type:** if your bike has rim brakes, a front hub motor kit adds significant weight to the front wheel — upgrade to disc brakes or use a rear hub kit instead.
7. **Disc brake rotor size:** some hub motors require a specific rotor mount (6-bolt or centre-lock). Check compatibility.

### Battery Selection

- **Voltage:** 36 V (standard for 250 W kits) or 48 V (more punch, but check controller compatibility)
- **Capacity:** 10 Ah minimum (360 Wh at 36 V). 14–17 Ah (500–620 Wh) is better for practical commuting range.
- **Cell quality:** Samsung, LG, or Panasonic cells. Avoid no-name cells — they degrade faster and pose a fire risk.
- **BMS:** must have a battery management system for overcharge, over-discharge, short circuit, and temperature protection.

## Installation Overview

### Hub Motor Kit (Front — Easiest)

**Tools needed:** Allen keys, adjustable spanner, zip ties, electrical tape, possibly a pedal wrench.
**Time:** 2–4 hours for a first-timer.

1. Remove the front wheel
2. Install the hub motor wheel (slot into the fork dropouts, secure the axle nuts)
3. Install the torque arm (prevents the axle spinning in the fork — critical safety item)
4. Mount the battery on the frame (down tube mount or rear rack mount)
5. Mount the display on the handlebars
6. Mount the throttle (if included) on the handlebars
7. Install the pedal assist sensor on the bottom bracket
8. Route and connect all cables (motor → controller → battery → display → sensor)
9. Secure all cables with zip ties (no loose cables near the wheels or chain)
10. Test: turn on the display, select an assist level, pedal — the motor should engage

### Mid-Drive Kit (Bafang BBS Series)

**Tools needed:** bottom bracket tool (specific to your BB type), crank puller, Allen keys, torque wrench, lock ring tool, chain breaker.
**Time:** 4–8 hours for a first-timer.

1. Remove the cranks and bottom bracket
2. Install the mid-drive motor unit into the BB shell
3. Install the chainring (provided with the kit)
4. Install the cranks onto the motor spindle
5. Mount the battery
6. Mount the display and controls
7. Route and connect all cables
8. Adjust the chain length (the new chainring may be a different size)
9. Test shifting through all gears under power

## Common Mistakes

1. **Skipping the torque arm (hub motor).** The torque arm prevents the motor axle from spinning in the dropout. Without it, the axle can strip the dropout — causing the wheel to detach under power. This is a safety-critical component.
2. **Buying an overpowered kit.** A 1,000 W kit on a bike with rim brakes and a lightweight frame is dangerous. The brakes cannot stop the speed, and the frame is not designed for the forces. Match the kit to the bike.
3. **Ignoring battery quality.** Cheap batteries with no-name cells are the most common cause of e-bike fires. Buy from a reputable supplier with Samsung/LG/Panasonic cells and a proper BMS.
4. **Poor cable routing.** Loose cables catch in the wheel, chain, or brakes. Route cables neatly with zip ties and check clearance at full handlebar turn.
5. **Not adjusting brakes.** The added weight and speed of an e-bike require stronger brakes. Adjust your brakes after installation — tighten pads, check rotor alignment, test stopping distance.

## Bottom Line

An e-bike conversion kit is a cost-effective way to electrify a bike you already own. A front hub motor kit is the easiest DIY project ($500–$800, 2–4 hours). A mid-drive kit offers better performance but requires more skill and tools ($700–$1,500, 4–8 hours). Either way, use quality cells (Samsung/LG/Panasonic), install the torque arm, restrict the motor to 250 W / 25 km/h for legal road use, and test thoroughly before riding in traffic.
