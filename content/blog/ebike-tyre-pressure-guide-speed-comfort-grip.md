Tyre pressure is the single most impactful adjustment you can make to your e-bike without spending a dollar. It affects speed, comfort, grip, puncture resistance, and battery range. Most riders set their pressure once and never touch it again — leaving significant performance on the table.

## The Basics: What the Numbers Mean

Every tyre has a pressure range printed on the sidewall — for example, "40–65 PSI" or "2.8–4.5 BAR." This is the manufacturer's recommended range for that tyre.

- **PSI** (pounds per square inch) is the most common unit in Australia
- **BAR** is the metric equivalent (1 BAR = 14.5 PSI)
- The **minimum** is the lowest safe pressure before pinch flats and rim damage
- The **maximum** is the highest safe pressure before blowout risk

Your ideal pressure is somewhere in this range, depending on your weight, terrain, weather, and riding priorities.

## How Pressure Affects Performance

### Higher Pressure (Closer to Maximum)

- **Faster rolling speed** on smooth, sealed roads — less tyre deformation = less rolling resistance
- **More responsive handling** — the tyre does not squirm under cornering forces
- **Less comfortable** — every bump, crack, and seam transmits through the tyre to your body
- **Less grip** on loose or wet surfaces — the smaller contact patch has fewer rubber molecules touching the ground
- **Higher puncture resistance** — a firmer tyre is harder for debris to penetrate

### Lower Pressure (Closer to Minimum)

- **More comfortable** — the tyre absorbs bumps and vibrations like a natural suspension system
- **More grip** on wet, loose, or uneven surfaces — the larger contact patch wraps around surface irregularities
- **Slower rolling** on smooth surfaces — more tyre deformation = more energy lost as heat
- **Higher pinch flat risk** — the tyre can compress against the rim when hitting a sharp edge (a "snake bite" puncture)
- **More battery drain** — rolling resistance is the largest energy consumer at low speeds

## Recommended Pressures by Riding Style

These are starting points. Adjust based on how the bike feels and your specific conditions.

### Road Commuting (700c or 28" tyres, 28–42 mm width)

| Rider weight | Recommended PSI |
|---|---|
| Under 65 kg | 55–65 PSI |
| 65–85 kg | 60–75 PSI |
| 85–100 kg | 70–85 PSI |
| Over 100 kg | 80–max PSI |

### Urban Commuting (26" or 27.5" tyres, 1.75–2.2" width)

| Rider weight | Recommended PSI |
|---|---|
| Under 65 kg | 35–45 PSI |
| 65–85 kg | 40–55 PSI |
| 85–100 kg | 50–60 PSI |
| Over 100 kg | 55–max PSI |

### Fat Tyre (26" × 4.0–5.0")

| Surface | Recommended PSI |
|---|---|
| Sealed roads | 20–30 PSI |
| Gravel / fire trails | 12–20 PSI |
| Sand (hard-packed) | 8–12 PSI |
| Sand (soft) | 5–8 PSI |

### Mountain Bike (27.5" or 29" × 2.2–2.6")

| Riding style | Front PSI | Rear PSI |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-country | 28–35 | 30–38 |
| Trail | 24–30 | 26–33 |
| Enduro / aggressive | 22–28 | 24–30 |

Note: MTB riders often run slightly lower pressure in the front tyre for better cornering grip, and slightly higher in the rear for climbing traction and pinch-flat resistance (more weight on the rear wheel).

## Temperature and Pressure

Tyre pressure changes with temperature. Air expands when heated and contracts when cooled.

- **A 10 °C temperature increase adds approximately 1–2 PSI**
- **A 10 °C decrease drops approximately 1–2 PSI**

In Australian conditions, this matters:
- A morning commute at 10 °C and an afternoon return at 35 °C means your pressure has increased by 3–5 PSI during the day
- In summer, if you inflate to maximum PSI in the cool morning, the tyre may exceed its rated maximum by afternoon
- In winter, tyres inflated in a warm garage will lose 2–4 PSI by the time you reach your cold morning commute

**Rule of thumb:** in summer, inflate 3–5 PSI below maximum. In winter, inflate to the upper end of your target range to compensate for cold-weather pressure drop.

## How Often to Check Pressure

- **Weekly** for commuter and urban e-bikes with standard tubes
- **Before every ride** for mountain biking and fat tyre riding (where small pressure changes make a big difference)
- **Monthly** for tubeless setups (tubeless tyres hold pressure longer than tubed tyres)

A floor pump with a built-in pressure gauge is the most accurate and easiest tool. Avoid gas station air compressors — they deliver high volume and can over-inflate a bicycle tyre in seconds.

## Tubeless vs Tubes

**Tubed tyres** are the default on most e-bikes. A rubber inner tube sits inside the tyre and holds the air. Punctures require removing the tube and patching or replacing it.

**Tubeless tyres** seal directly to the rim with no inner tube. A liquid sealant inside the tyre plugs small punctures automatically (thorns, glass, small nails). Advantages:
- Self-sealing small punctures (up to ~6 mm)
- Can run lower pressure without pinch flat risk (no tube to pinch)
- Slightly lower rolling resistance (no tube friction)

Disadvantages:
- Requires tubeless-compatible rims and tyres
- Sealant dries out and needs topping up every 3–6 months
- Large punctures still require a plug kit or a spare tube
- Initial setup can be fiddly — a tubeless tyre can be stubborn to seat on the rim

For commuters, standard tubes with puncture-resistant tyres are the simplest and most reliable option. For mountain bikers and fat tyre riders, tubeless is the better choice — the ability to run lower pressures without pinch flats is a significant performance advantage.

## Bottom Line

Check your tyre pressure weekly. Start with the recommended pressure for your weight and riding style, then adjust based on comfort and grip. Lower pressure for comfort and traction, higher pressure for speed and puncture resistance. A $30 floor pump with a gauge is the best tool you can buy for your e-bike — it costs nothing to use and improves every ride.
