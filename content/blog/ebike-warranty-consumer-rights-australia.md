An e-bike is a significant purchase — $2,000 to $6,000 for most models. When something goes wrong (and on a machine with electrical, mechanical, and electronic components, things eventually do), your warranty and consumer rights determine whether you pay for the fix or the seller does.

## Australian Consumer Law (ACL) — Your Baseline Rights

Regardless of any warranty the seller offers, Australian Consumer Law guarantees that products must be:

1. **Of acceptable quality:** safe, durable, free from defects, and acceptable in appearance. An e-bike should not have a motor that fails after 200 km or a battery that dies after 50 charges.
2. **Fit for purpose:** the bike must do what it is marketed to do. If it is sold as a commuter e-bike, it must handle daily commuting. If it is sold as waterproof, it must handle rain.
3. **Match the description:** the bike must match the specifications, images, and claims made by the seller. If the listing says "500 Wh battery, 80 km range," the bike must deliver that under reasonable conditions.

If a product fails to meet these guarantees, you are entitled to a **remedy** — repair, replacement, or refund — depending on the severity of the failure.

### Major Failure vs Minor Failure

**Major failure:** the product has a serious problem that cannot be fixed, or would not have been purchased if the buyer had known about it. Examples:
- Battery catches fire or swells dangerously
- Frame cracks within the first year
- Motor fails completely and cannot be repaired
- The bike is not road-legal despite being sold as compliant

**Remedy for major failure:** your choice of a full refund or a replacement of equal value.

**Minor failure:** the product has a problem that can be fixed within a reasonable time. Examples:
- Display stops working
- Brakes need adjustment after delivery
- A spoke breaks during the warranty period
- Software bug in the motor controller

**Remedy for minor failure:** the seller must repair the product within a reasonable time. If they cannot, you can request a replacement or refund.

### How Long Do ACL Rights Last?

There is no fixed time limit. ACL rights last for a "reasonable" period — which depends on the product type, price, and what a reasonable consumer would expect.

For an e-bike:
- A $3,000 commuter e-bike would reasonably be expected to last 3–5 years with normal use
- A $500 budget e-scooter might be expected to last 1–2 years
- A motor or battery failure after 6 months on any e-bike is clearly within the reasonable period

**Important:** ACL rights exist independently of the manufacturer's warranty. A manufacturer warranty that says "1 year" does not limit your ACL rights. If the motor fails after 18 months on a $4,000 bike, you still have a valid claim under ACL, even if the warranty has expired.

## Manufacturer Warranties

Most e-bike brands offer a specific warranty alongside your ACL rights:

| Component | Typical warranty |
|---|---|
| Frame | 2–5 years (lifetime on some premium brands) |
| Motor | 2–3 years |
| Battery | 1–2 years |
| Display and controller | 1–2 years |
| Mechanical components (brakes, gears, wheels) | 1 year |
| Wear items (tyres, brake pads, chain) | Not warranted |

### What Warranties Typically Cover

- Manufacturing defects (faulty welds, motor failure under normal use, battery cells that die prematurely)
- Component failure under normal use and proper maintenance
- Electrical faults in the motor, controller, or wiring harness

### What Warranties Typically Do NOT Cover

- **Normal wear and tear:** brake pads, tyres, chain, cassette, grips
- **Crash damage:** if you crash the bike, warranty does not cover the repair
- **Water damage beyond the IP rating:** if the motor is rated IP54 and you submerge it, the warranty does not apply
- **Modifications:** if you install aftermarket components, flash custom firmware, or modify the motor, the warranty may be voided for the affected components
- **Commercial use:** some warranties exclude or limit coverage for delivery riders and rental fleets (check the terms)
- **Improper maintenance:** if you never service the bike and the chain breaks, taking the motor with it, that is not a warranty claim

## What to Do When Something Goes Wrong

### Step 1: Document the Problem

- Take photos and videos of the fault
- Note the date the problem appeared
- Record your total distance ridden (most displays show an odometer)
- Save your purchase receipt and any warranty documentation

### Step 2: Contact the Seller (Not Just the Manufacturer)

Under ACL, your contract is with the **seller** — the shop or online retailer who sold you the bike. The seller is responsible for providing a remedy, even if the fault is in a component made by a different manufacturer.

The seller may refer you to the manufacturer for warranty service, but they cannot refuse to deal with you. If the manufacturer is overseas and unresponsive, the seller is still your point of contact.

### Step 3: Be Specific About the Remedy You Want

- For a minor failure: "I would like this repaired under warranty."
- For a major failure: "I would like a refund" or "I would like a replacement."

You do not need to accept a repair if the failure is major. You do not need to accept a credit note instead of a refund if you are entitled to one.

### Step 4: Escalate If Necessary

If the seller refuses a reasonable remedy:
1. Put your request in writing (email) with a clear description of the fault, photos, and a reference to your ACL rights
2. Contact your state's consumer affairs body: Consumer Affairs Victoria, NSW Fair Trading, Office of Fair Trading (QLD), etc.
3. Lodge a complaint with the ACCC if the seller is engaging in misleading conduct
4. Consider the Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (VCAT) or equivalent in your state for disputes under $10,000

## Tips for Protecting Yourself

1. **Keep your receipt** and all warranty documentation (digital copies are fine)
2. **Register your bike** with the manufacturer if they offer warranty registration
3. **Follow the maintenance schedule** — a well-maintained bike is easier to claim warranty on
4. **Keep records of servicing** (receipts from bike shops, notes on home maintenance)
5. **Buy from an Australian retailer** — Australian Consumer Law applies to Australian sellers. Buying directly from an overseas seller on AliExpress or Alibaba gives you no ACL protection.
6. **Read the warranty terms** before buying — some brands have better warranty support than others

## Bottom Line

Your strongest protection is Australian Consumer Law, not the manufacturer's warranty. ACL guarantees that your e-bike must be of acceptable quality and fit for purpose — and this protection lasts for a reasonable period that is often longer than the written warranty. If something goes wrong, contact the seller, document the fault, and be clear about the remedy you want. A $3,000 e-bike that fails after 12 months is not acceptable quality, regardless of what the warranty card says.
