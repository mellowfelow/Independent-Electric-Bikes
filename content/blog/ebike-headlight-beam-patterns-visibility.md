A headlight is not just a headlight. The beam pattern — where the light goes and where it does not — determines whether you can see the road, whether oncoming traffic is blinded, and whether you are visible from the side.

## Beam Pattern Types

### Flood Beam

A wide, even spread of light covering a broad area in front of the bike.

**Best for:** urban commuting on lit streets. The wide spread illuminates the road surface, kerbs, and objects at the edges of your vision. It also makes you visible from a wide angle — drivers at intersections can see the light from the side.

**Weakness:** less focused forward throw. On an unlit path, a flood beam illuminates the ground 10–15 metres ahead but may not show you what is 30 metres away.

### Spot Beam

A concentrated, focused beam that throws light far down the road.

**Best for:** rural riding, unlit bike paths, and high-speed descents where you need to see far ahead. A spot beam can illuminate objects 50–100 metres away.

**Weakness:** narrow — you see what is straight ahead but miss objects at the edges. On a shared path, oncoming cyclists and pedestrians outside the beam are invisible until they are right in front of you. Also, a spot beam aimed slightly too high blinds oncoming traffic.

### Shaped Beam (StVZO / ECE Compliant)

A precisely engineered beam pattern with a sharp horizontal cut-off at the top. Below the cut-off, the beam is bright and wide. Above it, virtually no light escapes.

This design comes from automotive headlight standards — German StVZO (road traffic regulations) and European ECE standards require bicycle lights to not blind oncoming traffic, just like car headlights.

**Best for:** shared-path riding, any situation where you face oncoming cyclists or pedestrians. The cut-off means you illuminate the road without blinding anyone coming toward you. This is the most considerate and safest beam pattern for shared infrastructure.

**Weakness:** less raw brightness above the cut-off line. On a completely unlit trail where there is no oncoming traffic, a spot beam shows you more of what is coming.

**Brands specialising in shaped beams:** Busch + Müller (B&M), Lupine, Supernova, Lezyne (some models).

## Lumens vs Lux

### Lumens
Total light output from the light. More lumens = more light in total. But lumens do not tell you where the light goes — 800 lumens in a flood pattern and 800 lumens in a spot beam look completely different on the road.

### Lux
Light intensity at a specific point (measured at a defined distance). Lux tells you how bright the light is where it matters — on the road surface ahead of you. A light with 50 lux at 10 metres illuminates the road better than a light with 30 lux at 10 metres, even if the lower-lux light has more total lumens (because the lumens are spread wider).

**What matters for road illumination:** lux at 10 metres. This is the standard measurement for StVZO lights and gives a meaningful comparison between lights.

**What matters for being seen:** lumens (total output). More lumens = more visible to drivers from any angle.

**For commuting, you need both:** enough lux to see the road, and enough lumens to be seen by traffic.

## Mounting Position

Where you mount the light changes how the beam hits the road.

### Handlebar Mount (Standard)
The light sits 90–100 cm above the ground. The beam angle is relatively flat — it illuminates the road 10–30 metres ahead without much glare upward.

**Best for:** most commuting. Easy to aim, easy to remove for charging, easy to adjust.

### Helmet Mount
The light follows your head — look into a corner, the light follows. Look at a street sign, the light illuminates it.

**Best for:** mountain biking, trail riding, and as a secondary light alongside a handlebar-mounted primary. The ability to look where you need light is valuable off-road.

**Caution:** a helmet-mounted light aimed at oncoming traffic is blinding — the light is at eye level and follows your gaze. On shared paths, keep the helmet light aimed downward or switch it off when facing oncoming users.

### Fork or Low Mount
The light sits 30–40 cm above the ground, mounted on the fork leg or a front rack.

**Effect:** long shadows. Every bump, crack, and pothole in the road casts a long shadow toward you. This makes surface texture extremely visible — you see hazards that a high-mounted light would wash out.

**Best for:** revealing road surface details. Excellent for rough roads, bike paths with debris, and avoiding potholes.

**Limitation:** less forward throw and less visible to oncoming traffic (the light is below car-bumper height).

## Recommended Setup

### Urban Commuting (Lit Streets)
- **Primary:** handlebar-mounted flood beam, 400–600 lumens
- **Mode:** steady for main roads, flash for well-lit streets where being seen matters more than seeing

### Urban Commuting (Mixed Lit/Unlit)
- **Primary:** handlebar-mounted shaped beam (StVZO), 600–1000 lumens / 50+ lux
- **Secondary (optional):** fork-mounted light for road surface detail

### Suburban/Rural (Unlit Roads)
- **Primary:** handlebar-mounted flood or shaped beam, 800–1200 lumens / 70+ lux
- **Secondary:** fork-mounted or second handlebar light for surface detail
- **Total output:** 1200+ lumens across both lights

### Mountain Biking / Trail Riding
- **Primary:** handlebar-mounted spot or flood beam, 1200–2000 lumens
- **Secondary:** helmet-mounted spot beam, 600–1000 lumens
- **Total output:** 1800–3000 lumens (trails are unlit and full of obstacles)

## Flash Mode: Yes or No?

**For being seen:** flash mode is more attention-getting than steady. The movement catches a driver's peripheral vision. Use flash mode in daytime and on well-lit urban streets at night.

**For seeing the road:** steady mode only. Flash mode creates a strobe effect that makes it harder to judge distance, speed, and surface detail. On an unlit path, a flashing light is disorienting.

**Best practice:** if your light has a "pulse" mode (bright with periodic brighter flash), use that. It combines the attention-getting flash with the road-illuminating steady beam.

## Battery Life and Charging

At higher lumen outputs, battery life drops:
- 400 lumens: 3–6 hours
- 800 lumens: 2–4 hours
- 1200 lumens: 1–3 hours
- 2000 lumens: 1–2 hours

**For commuting:** choose a light that runs for at least 2 hours at your required brightness (covering your round trip with margin). USB-rechargeable lights can be charged at work.

**E-bike-powered lights:** some e-bikes have lights powered by the main battery. These never run out independently and are always on when the bike is on. The best solution for commuting — but you cannot take them off the bike for walking or non-e-bike use.

## Bottom Line

For most Australian commuters, a handlebar-mounted shaped beam (StVZO) light at 600–1000 lumens is the ideal choice — it illuminates the road without blinding oncoming cyclists and pedestrians. Add a fork-mounted light for surface detail on rough roads. Use flash mode in daytime and steady mode at night. Budget $60–$150 for a quality commuter headlight with USB charging.
