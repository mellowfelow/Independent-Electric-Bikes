Three-wheeled electric bikes — e-trikes — offer something that no two-wheeled bicycle can: stability at a standstill. You do not need to balance, you do not need to unclip or put a foot down at lights, and you do not fall over if you stop suddenly. For riders with balance difficulties, disabilities, or age-related stability concerns, an e-trike opens up independent cycling that a standard bicycle cannot.

## Who Rides E-Trikes?

E-trikes are not niche. They serve a wide range of riders:

- **Older adults** who have stopped riding two-wheeled bikes due to balance concerns
- **Riders recovering from injury or surgery** (hip replacement, knee reconstruction, stroke recovery)
- **People with neurological conditions** affecting balance (Parkinson's disease, vestibular disorders, MS)
- **Riders with physical disabilities** that make balancing a two-wheeler difficult or impossible
- **Cargo haulers** who want a stable platform for heavy loads
- **Anyone** who simply prefers the confidence of three wheels

## Trike Configurations

### Delta (Two Rear, One Front)

The most common configuration. Two wheels at the rear, one at the front. The rider steers with the front wheel like a standard bicycle.

**Advantages:**
- Familiar steering feel
- Easy to mount — step through the frame or sit down from the side
- Wide rear platform for baskets, cargo, or child seats
- Stable under heavy loads

**Disadvantages:**
- Wider turning radius than a bicycle
- Can tip on sharp corners if taken too fast (the inside rear wheel lifts)
- Wider than a bike lane in some locations

### Tadpole (Two Front, One Rear)

Two wheels at the front, one at the rear. Common on recumbent trikes.

**Advantages:**
- Lower centre of gravity (recumbent position)
- Better cornering stability — the two front wheels resist tipping
- Sportier, faster handling
- Aerodynamic (recumbent models)

**Disadvantages:**
- Less practical for cargo
- Harder to see in traffic (lower seating position)
- More complex steering (two front wheels need a linkage system)

For most utility and mobility riders, a **delta configuration** is the better choice. Tadpole trikes suit riders who want performance and already have good upper-body strength.

## Electric Assist on a Trike

The motor matters more on a trike than on a bike. Trikes are heavier (30–45 kg without the rider), have higher rolling resistance (three tyres instead of two), and are less aerodynamic. A motor that feels adequate on a 22 kg e-bike may feel underpowered on a 38 kg trike.

**Recommendations:**
- **Motor: 500 W+ hub motor or mid-drive.** A 250 W motor works on flat ground but struggles on hills with a heavy trike and rider.
- **Battery: 500 Wh+.** The extra rolling resistance means 20–30 % less range per watt-hour than a standard e-bike.
- **Torque: 50 Nm+** for comfortable hill climbing without maxing out the motor.

Most e-trikes use a **rear hub motor** on the delta configuration, driving one of the two rear wheels (single-drive) or both (differential drive). Differential drive is more stable under power but more expensive and complex.

## Practical Considerations

### Width

A standard e-trike is 70–85 cm wide. A regular bike is 45–55 cm. This means:

- **Bike lanes:** most Australian bike lanes are 1.2–1.5 m wide, so a trike fits — but with less room to manoeuvre around obstacles.
- **Shared paths:** a trike takes up more than half a standard 2.5 m shared path. Expect to slow down or stop for oncoming cyclists and pedestrians.
- **Storage:** you need garage or shed space. A trike does not fit in a standard hallway or through most doorways without turning.
- **Transport:** a trike does not fit on a standard car bike rack. You need a trailer, ute tray, or van.

### Speed

Trikes are slower than bikes at the same power level — higher weight, more rolling resistance, and more aerodynamic drag. Expect 15–22 km/h cruising speed versus 22–28 km/h on a two-wheeler.

### Cornering

The biggest handling difference. On a bicycle, you lean into corners. On a trike, you do not lean — the outside rear wheel takes the load. Take corners slowly until you learn the trike's limits. Most tipping incidents happen when riders corner too fast, especially downhill.

**Rule of thumb:** if you feel the inside wheel lifting, you are going too fast for that corner.

### Terrain

Trikes handle flat, sealed surfaces and gentle gravel well. They are less suited to:
- Steep, narrow singletrack (too wide)
- Deep sand or mud (three wheels = more friction)
- Very steep descents (harder to control speed with three braking surfaces)

## Choosing an E-Trike

1. **Test ride before buying.** Trike handling is completely different from a bicycle. What feels stable at a standstill may feel unfamiliar in motion. Spend 20+ minutes on a test ride.
2. **Prioritise the motor and battery.** Under-powered trikes are frustrating — the extra weight and drag demand adequate motor torque.
3. **Check the seat and back support.** Many trikes have a seat with a backrest — essential for riders with back issues. Ensure the seat height and position are adjustable.
4. **Consider cargo capacity.** Delta trikes often have a large rear basket. If you plan to carry groceries or supplies, check the weight rating.
5. **Measure your storage space.** Know the trike's dimensions before buying.

## Bottom Line

E-trikes are the most accessible form of electric cycling. They remove the balance requirement, support riders with a wide range of physical abilities, and carry heavy loads on a stable platform. The trade-offs — wider, heavier, slower — are genuine, but for riders who cannot safely ride a two-wheeler, an e-trike is not a compromise. It is the right tool.
