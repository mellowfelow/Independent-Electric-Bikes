How long does an e-bike motor last? Longer than the battery, longer than the drivetrain, and longer than most people keep the bike. Here is what wears out, what does not, and how to maximise motor life.

## Expected Lifespan

### Hub Motors
- **Direct-drive hub motor:** 15,000–30,000+ km (10–20 years of average use). No wearing internal parts — only the bearings can fail, and they are replaceable.
- **Geared hub motor:** 10,000–20,000 km before the internal nylon reduction gears wear out. Gear replacement costs $30–$80 in parts (done by a bike shop or a confident home mechanic). With gear replacements, the motor itself lasts 20,000–40,000+ km.

### Mid-Drive Motors
- **Premium (Bosch, Shimano, Brose, TQ):** 15,000–25,000+ km. These are engineered for longevity with hardened steel gears, sealed bearings, and robust housings.
- **Budget mid-drive (Bafang BBS01/BBS02):** 8,000–15,000 km. Lighter-duty internals wear faster, especially under heavy loads and high assist levels.

### For Context
The average Australian e-bike rider covers 2,000–5,000 km per year. At that rate:
- A hub motor lasts 5–15+ years
- A mid-drive motor lasts 3–12+ years
- A battery lasts 2–5 years (500–1,000 cycles)
- A chain lasts 3,000–5,000 km (1–2 years)

**The motor is almost always the longest-lasting major component on the bike.**

## What Wears Out

### Bearings
Every motor has bearings — two or more ball bearings that allow the rotor to spin freely inside the stator. Bearings wear due to:
- Normal rotation (every bearing has a lifespan measured in hours or revolutions)
- Water ingress (riding through deep puddles, pressure washing)
- Contamination (dust, grit entering the bearing seal)
- Overloading (carrying heavy loads up steep hills at low speed stresses bearings)

**Symptoms of worn bearings:**
- Grinding or rumbling noise from the motor
- Increased resistance when spinning the wheel by hand (hub motor)
- Play (wobble) in the motor shaft

**Replacement cost:** $20–$50 for bearings, $50–$100 labour at a bike shop. Bearings are standard industrial sizes — readily available.

### Internal Gears (Geared Hub Motors)
Geared hub motors use nylon or composite planetary gears to reduce motor speed and increase torque. These gears wear over time:
- Under normal use: 10,000–20,000 km before noticeable wear
- Under heavy use (steep hills, heavy loads, high assist): 5,000–10,000 km
- Symptoms: clicking noise under load, reduced power delivery, motor spins but wheel does not drive (stripped gears)

**Replacement cost:** $30–$80 for gear kit, $50–$100 labour. Most geared hub motors are designed for gear replacement — it is a maintenance item, not a failure.

### Clutch (Geared Hub Motors)
The one-way clutch (freewheel mechanism) allows the wheel to spin freely when you coast. It can wear or fail:
- Symptoms: motor drags when coasting (you feel resistance), or motor engages intermittently
- Replacement cost: $20–$40 for the clutch, replaced during a gear service

### Controller
The controller (the electronic board that manages power delivery) is solid-state — no moving parts. It rarely fails. When it does:
- Cause: overheating (riding at maximum power up a long, steep hill in hot weather), water damage, or voltage spike
- Symptoms: motor cuts out intermittently, error codes on the display, motor runs at full power regardless of assist level (dangerous — stop immediately)
- Replacement cost: $100–$300 for the controller

## What Does NOT Wear Out

### Stator and Rotor
The motor's main components — the copper coil windings (stator) and the permanent magnets (rotor) — do not wear under normal use. Copper does not fatigue from electrical current, and permanent magnets retain their strength for decades.

**The only thing that kills a stator is overheating** (melting the insulation on the copper windings). This happens only under extreme abuse: sustained maximum power output in high ambient temperatures, or running the motor while physically stalled (wheel cannot turn, motor draws maximum current).

### Motor Housing
The aluminium housing is structural — it does not wear. It can be damaged by a crash impact, but normal riding does not affect it.

## Maximising Motor Lifespan

### Use Appropriate Assist Levels
Running in Turbo/maximum assist all the time puts maximum stress on the motor. For flat ground and gentle hills, use Eco or Tour mode. Save Turbo for steep climbs where you need it.

**The motor works harder at low cadence (pedalling slowly) and high torque.** Shift to a lower gear before the hill — the motor runs more efficiently at higher RPM.

### Avoid Sustained Maximum Load
Climbing a long, steep hill at low speed in maximum assist is the hardest work a motor does. The motor draws maximum current, generates maximum heat, and the bearings carry maximum load.

**Mitigation:**
- Shift to a very low gear (the motor turns faster but with less strain)
- Alternate between Turbo and Sport on long climbs (gives the motor brief rest periods)
- Stop and let the motor cool if you feel significant heat from the motor housing on a very long climb

### Keep Water Out
Water ingress is the most common preventable cause of motor damage:
- Do not ride through water deeper than the motor housing (hub motor: axle height; mid-drive: bottom bracket height)
- Do not pressure-wash the motor. Use a gentle hose or a damp cloth.
- If you ride through deep water accidentally, spin the wheel freely for a minute afterwards to fling water out of the bearing area

### Mid-Drive: Protect the Drivetrain
A mid-drive motor's force goes through the chain and cassette. A dirty, dry, or worn chain increases friction and stress on the motor's output shaft.
- Clean and lubricate the chain every 100–200 km
- Replace the chain at 0.5 % wear (before it damages the cassette)
- Shift under load (do not mash a high gear up a steep hill — shift down before the gradient increases)

### Software Updates
Manufacturers release firmware updates that can improve motor efficiency, reduce noise, and fix bugs that cause unnecessary wear. Check for updates through the manufacturer's app (Bosch eBike Flow, Shimano E-Tube, etc.) every 6–12 months.

## When to Replace vs Repair

| Issue | Repair or Replace? | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Worn bearings | Repair (replace bearings) | $70–$150 |
| Worn gears (geared hub) | Repair (replace gear kit) | $80–$180 |
| Worn clutch (geared hub) | Repair (replace clutch) | $70–$140 |
| Failed controller | Replace controller | $100–$300 |
| Overheated stator (burnt windings) | Replace motor | $300–$800 |
| Crash damage to housing | Replace motor | $300–$800 |
| Obsolete motor (no parts available) | Replace motor or bike | Varies |

**Most motor issues are repairable.** Full motor replacement is rarely needed before 15,000–20,000 km on a quality system.

## Warranty

Most e-bike motors carry a 2-year manufacturer warranty. Some brands offer extended warranties:
- **Bosch:** 2 years (extendable)
- **Shimano STEPS:** 2 years
- **Bafang:** 1–2 years (varies by retailer)

Warranty typically covers manufacturing defects — not wear items (bearings, gears), water damage from abuse, or crash damage.

## Bottom Line

An e-bike motor lasts 10,000–30,000+ km — far longer than the battery (500–1,000 cycles), chain (3,000–5,000 km), or brake pads (2,000–5,000 km). Bearings and internal gears are the main wear items, and both are cheap to replace ($70–$180). To maximise lifespan: use appropriate assist levels, shift to low gears on hills, keep the chain clean (mid-drive), keep water out of the motor, and do not pressure-wash. The motor is the most durable major component on your e-bike — take basic care of it and it will outlast everything else.
